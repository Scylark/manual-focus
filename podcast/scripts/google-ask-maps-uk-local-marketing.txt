HANNAH: Hi James. Mostly boring things. First, what Google said.

JAMES: [calmly] Go on then. What actually changed?

HANNAH: Ask Maps launched back in March, in the US and India. You type a question in plain language and Maps answers it, drawing on more than three hundred million places and reviews from over five hundred million contributors, according to Google's launch post. The new post, from Maz Khorasani on the Maps team, takes it to thirty countries in Europe, including Spain, Germany and the UK, and more than two hundred countries and territories in all.

JAMES: And the new features?

HANNAH: Four that matter here. Trip itineraries, so a day of Christmas shopping or a coastal road trip over several days comes back as a schedule with numbered stops. Live help once you're on the way, showing traffic, whether each stop is open and what the next one's like. Searching your saved places against live availability, so Google's example is which saved spots can seat five people within half an hour. And then the agentic part, appointment finding.

JAMES: Which of those reach the UK first?

HANNAH: Itineraries and saved-list search work everywhere Ask Maps is offered, so those come with it. Appointment finding, and new route tips for parking and tolls, launch first in the US, with other regions to follow.

JAMES: [thoughtfully] Tell me about the booking, because that's the bit that sounds like an agent.

HANNAH: It is. In the US, Ask Maps can find open slots at beauty, pet care, auto repair and home repair businesses, and call local businesses on the user's behalf. Google's Business Profile help page fills in the rest. It says Google may phone a business to book an appointment or check prices and availability, and if the business uses an online booking provider, Google may book through that instead.

JAMES: Can a business say no?

HANNAH: A verified one can. In Business Profile, under advanced settings, there's a toggle called Bookings and inquiries from customers. Staff can also ask a Google caller to stop. What Google's Maps post doesn't say is whether Ask Maps calls follow that toggle.

JAMES: So here's the question a head of marketing with forty branches will ask. How does Ask Maps decide which of my branches to mention?

HANNAH: Google hasn't said. It names the inputs, which are places data, community reviews and the user's own searches and saved places. It doesn't say how one business gets picked over another. So the levers you've got are your listing and your reviews.

JAMES: [dryly] Which is local SEO with a chat window on the front.

HANNAH: Partly. The customer asks for an outcome, a four o'clock nail appointment or a day out, and gets one plan back. If your branch isn't in it, they never saw a list with you on it.

JAMES: And we've seen how that tends to go. When we covered the Lightspeed study in September, AI assistants picked the national chain over a smaller rival up to ninety-four per cent of the time in head-to-head comparisons.

HANNAH: And nothing in Google's posts says Maps will treat independents differently. The other thread is last week, when OpenAI opened Businesses in ChatGPT. That turned the ChatGPT profile into source material for an AI answer, and Google's posts don't name Business Profiles, but the details Ask Maps shows, like whether a place is open, are the ones you set there.

JAMES: [thoughtfully] So our view is that a UK brand with physical locations should treat Business Profile accuracy as a December priority.
