Strategy, campaign, creative and execution are four layers of the same marketing work. Strategy is the choice of who you serve and why they should pick you. A campaign is a time-bound push that carries part of that strategy. Creative is the idea people see and hear. Execution is the delivery. Teams that blur them argue about the wrong things.

## Key takeaways

- Strategy decides who, what change and why you. It usually holds for a year or more and outlives many campaigns.
- A campaign has its own objective, budget, start date and end date, and should name the part of the strategy it serves.
- Creative is the idea and the work the audience meets. Seth Godin's book Purple Cow argues that work which isn't remarkable goes unnoticed.
- Execution is delivery, the assets, media, owners and checks. Measure it separately from results, or a good idea gets blamed for a missed deadline.
- Godin's smallest viable audience and "people like us do things like this" are strategy ideas, so they belong before the campaign brief.

## Why the four words get confused

In most marketing meetings the four words get used interchangeably. A head of marketing says "our strategy this quarter is the summer sale", an agency calls a set of social posts "the creative strategy", and a board asks why the "campaign" didn't work when the real problem was that half the assets went live a week late.

The confusion has a cost. When strategy and campaign blur, every new idea looks like a change of direction. When creative and execution blur, nobody can tell whether a poor result came from the idea or the delivery. Separating them gives each conversation the right people and the right test.

| Layer | The question it answers | Typical time span | Who owns it | How to judge it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy | Who do we serve, what change do we want, why us? | A year or more | Marketing leadership with the business | Did the business results move? |
| Campaign | What push carries part of the strategy now? | Weeks to months | Campaign or channel lead | Did it hit its own objective? |
| Creative | What idea will the audience notice and remember? | Life of the campaign, sometimes years | Creative lead or agency | Was it noticed, understood and talked about? |
| Execution | How does the work get made and delivered? | Days to weeks per piece | Producers, channel managers | On time, on brief, error-free? |

## What is a marketing strategy?

A marketing strategy is a set of choices. It names the audience you'll serve, the change you want in their behaviour, and the reason they should choose you over the alternatives, including doing nothing. In [SOSTAC](/blog/sostac-marketing-plan-basics/) terms it is the S that follows Situation and Objectives, the "how do we get there" stage.

Seth Godin's work is useful here because it insists on the choice. In [This Is Marketing](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/301349/this-is-marketing-by-godin-seth/9780241370148), published in 2018, he asks marketers to identify their smallest viable audience, and on [his blog](https://seths.blog/2022/05/the-smallest-viable-audience/) he describes that small, well-chosen group as a stepping-stone rather than a compromise. The idea is to pick the people you can serve so well that they recommend you, then grow from there.

Godin's other strategy idea is "people like us do things like this", the title of a [2017 essay](https://seths.blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/2017-people-like-us.pdf) he wrote for altMBA students. His argument is that people choose based on the group they see themselves in, so culture and belonging drive behaviour more than features do. For a strategist it turns "who is our audience" into "which group do they belong to, and what do people in that group do".

Our view at Manual Focus is that most weak campaigns are strategy problems in disguise, because a campaign briefed without a clear audience and reason to choose has nothing for the creative to sharpen. The [positioning audit pipeline](/lens/brand/positioning-audit-pipeline/) is a quick way to test whether your strategy is a choice or a wish.

## How is a campaign different from strategy?

A campaign is a time-bound push. It has one objective, a budget, dates and a defined audience, and it carries part of the strategy into the market. A strategy might run several campaigns a year, a launch, a seasonal push and an always-on acquisition programme, all serving the same choice of audience and position.

Les Binet and Peter Field's IPA report [The Long and the Short of It](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-balancing-short-and-long-term-marketing-strategies) looked at how campaign effects develop over time, and warned against relying on short-term online metrics as the main measure of success. That is a useful check on any campaign plan. A campaign judged only on this month's clicks will crowd out the brand-building work a strategy needs over years.

A good campaign brief names the strategy line it serves. If it can't, either the campaign shouldn't run or the strategy has a gap. The [message house generator](/lens/brand/message-house-generator/) turns a strategy into the core messages each campaign can draw on.

## What counts as creative?

Creative is the idea and the work the audience meets, the concept, the words, the images, the sound and the design. Agencies use the word as a noun for both the finished ads and the team that makes them.

Godin's [Purple Cow](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/54880/purple-cow-by-godin-seth/9780141924861) makes the case for remarkable work. A brown cow in a field goes unseen, a purple one gets talked about, and Godin argues the product or idea itself should be the thing worth remarking on, rather than a dull offer wrapped in loud advertising. For creative work that sets the test. Will the audience notice this, and will they mention it to someone?

Judge creative against the strategy, not taste. An idea everyone in the room likes can still miss the audience the strategy chose. The [tagline system](/lens/brand/tagline-system/) playbook shows how to generate and test lines against a brief rather than by vote.

## What does execution cover?

Execution is delivery. It covers producing the assets, placing the media, publishing the content, running the events and checking each piece before and after it goes live. In agency language an "execution" is also one version of an idea, so a campaign might have three executions, a film, a poster and a social cut.

Execution is where AI changes the most today. Drafting variants, resizing assets, checking copy against brand rules and building weekly reports are all jobs that sit at the second of the [three layers of AI](/blog/three-layers-of-ai-basics/). The [brief-to-ship pipeline](/lens/ops/brief-to-ship-pipeline/) shows how one team's execution runs from brief to publication with checks at each step.

Measure execution on its own terms. Was it on time, on brief and free of errors? If you only measure results, a strong idea delivered late looks like a weak idea, and the team learns the wrong lesson.

## How the four layers connect

Each layer should trace back to the one above it. Every piece of execution delivers part of a creative idea, every creative idea serves a campaign objective, and every campaign carries part of the strategy. When a result disappoints, walk back up the chain in that order. Check execution first, because it is the cheapest to fix, then the creative, then the campaign, and question the strategy last.
