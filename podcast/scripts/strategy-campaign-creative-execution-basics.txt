JAMES: Four words get used interchangeably in nearly every marketing meeting: strategy, campaign, creative and execution. And when they blur, teams end up arguing about the wrong thing, usually blaming the idea for what was really a missed deadline. I'm James Vickers, this is Manual Focus, and in this Basics episode Hannah and I are pulling the four apart.

A campaign is a time-bound push that carries part of that strategy into the market. Creative is the idea and the work the audience actually sees and hears. And execution is the delivery.

HANNAH: And the time spans are different, aren't they? That's what made it click for me in the article.

JAMES: A set of choices. The audience you'll serve, the change you want in what they do, and the reason they should choose you over the alternatives, including doing nothing. In the SOSTAC model we covered in the last Basics episode, it's the S that comes after Situation and Objectives. How do we get there?

HANNAH: And this is where you wanted to bring in Seth Godin.

JAMES: Because he's very good on the choice part. Do you want to explain the smallest viable audience? I think it lands better from first principles.

HANNAH: Sure. Godin sets it out in his twenty eighteen book, This Is Marketing, and he's written about it on his blog. The idea is that instead of trying to reach everyone, you pick the smallest group of people you could serve so well that the work sustains itself, and so well that they recommend you to people like them. On his blog he describes that small group as a stepping-stone rather than a compromise. You grow outwards from it.

JAMES: And the reason marketers resist it is that it feels like leaving money on the table.

HANNAH: Which is the opposite of how it works if he's right. A vague audience gives the creative nothing to aim at.

JAMES: That's what you'll usually see when a campaign underperforms. People go straight to the creative. Was the ad wrong? But when you look at the brief, the audience is "adults, twenty-five to fifty-four, interested in fitness". That isn't a choice. Our view is that most weak campaigns are strategy problems in disguise.

HANNAH: It's the title of an essay he wrote in twenty seventeen for students on his altMBA course. The argument is that people make choices based on the group they see themselves as part of, so culture and belonging drive behaviour more than features do. For a strategist, it changes the question from "who is our audience" to "which group do they belong to, and what do people in that group do".

JAMES: Which is very practical. If you're selling to club cyclists, the question isn't which features they want. It's what a serious club cyclist is expected to ride, wear and know about. Then you decide whether you're going to fit that, or change it.

HANNAH: So, campaigns. How is a campaign different?

JAMES: It's a push with a beginning and an end. One objective, a budget, dates and a defined audience, carrying part of the strategy into the market. One strategy might run several campaigns a year. A launch, a seasonal push, an always-on acquisition programme. All serving the same choice of audience and position.

HANNAH: The article brings in Binet and Field here.

JAMES: Les Binet and Peter Field's report for the IPA, The Long and the Short of It. They looked at how campaign effects build up over time, and they warned against relying on short-term online metrics as the main measure of success.

HANNAH: Why does that matter for the distinction we're making?

JAMES: Because a campaign judged only on this month's clicks will crowd out the brand-building work a strategy needs over years. If every campaign has to pay back in four weeks, you end up with a strategy nobody's actually funding.

HANNAH: And how do you check a campaign's in the right relationship with the strategy?

JAMES: The brief names the line of the strategy it serves. If it can't, either the campaign shouldn't run or the strategy has a gap. That's a very quick test, and it kills a lot of pet projects. JAMES: I'd treat it as one for planning purposes, with a review date instead of an end date. Search, email flows, a steady level of paid social. HANNAH: So the review date does the job the end date would have done.
