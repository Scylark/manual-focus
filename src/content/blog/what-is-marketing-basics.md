The Chartered Institute of Marketing defines marketing as the management process responsible for identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer requirements profitably. In practice that is one chain of decisions, from whether a market exists, through what you say and where you say it, to how you measure it. This guide follows the chain through one worked example, a new cycling helmet.

## Key takeaways

- The CIM defines marketing as the management process responsible for identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer requirements profitably.
- Marc Andreessen wrote in June 2007 that product/market fit means being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market.
- A communication hierarchy ranks what you say, and a benefits ladder climbs each message from feature to benefit to emotional reason.
- Forrester's 2009 report No Media Should Stand Alone argued that owned, paid and earned media work best together, with paid media as the catalyst.
- SOSTAC turns the whole chain, research and product through to channels, launch and measurement, into one plan.

## What is marketing?

The [CIM's definition](https://www.cim.co.uk/content-insights/articles/what-is-marketing/) is the one most UK courses teach. The institute says marketing decides what a company will produce, what it will charge, how it will deliver to the customer and how it will tell customers about it, summed up as the 7Ps of product, price, place, promotion, people, process and physical evidence. The [American Marketing Association](https://www.ama.org/the-definition-of-marketing-what-is-marketing/) describes it as the activity and processes for creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners and society.

Seth Godin offers a softer description. His 2018 book [This Is Marketing](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/600458/this-is-marketing-by-seth-godin) frames marketing as helping others become who they want to be, and argues that great marketers solve other people's problems rather than their company's. The CIM tells you what the job covers, and Godin tells you what it's for.

Promotion is one of the seven Ps, and it is the part most people see. It matters, but it works best at the end of the chain, once you know who you are talking to and what you are saying.

## Is there a market for a new helmet?

Take a hypothetical UK cycling brand planning a new road helmet. Andreessen's [2007 essay](https://pmarchive.com/guide_to_startups_part4.html) defined product/market fit as being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market, and called getting there the only thing that matters for a young company.

<figure class="dg dg-market"><div class="dg-head"><span class="dg-kicker">Step 1 · Product-market fit</span><span class="dg-title">Is there a market you can win?</span></div><div class="dg-split"><ol class="dg-list dg-funnel"><li style="--i:0"><span class="dg-strong">Riders who buy helmets</span><span class="dg-q">The category exists. Riders already buy helmets, so a market exists. The real question is narrower. Which riders are badly served by what they wear now, and would they switch at the price the brand needs? Ask what they do instead, too. For a commuter the alternative might be a clip-on light, or not riding after dark at all.

## What should the research find?

Research turns "cyclists" into people. It should find out who rides and where, what they wear and why, what they complain about, where they buy, and what triggers a purchase, whether a crash, a new bike or the clocks going back. It should end in three or four findings with evidence, as in the Situation stage of [SOSTAC](/blog/sostac-marketing-plan-basics/). Say the hypothetical research found that commuters want to be seen on dark evenings and road riders overheat on long climbs.

## From research to product requirements

A requirements brief written by marketing says what the product must do for the buyer, in the buyer's words. "Visible from behind on an unlit road" is a requirement, and an integrated rear light is one answer to it. Anything that traces to no finding is a feature looking for a reason.

## What is a communication hierarchy?

A communication hierarchy ranks what you say. One core message sits at the top, three or four supporting messages sit beneath it, and proof sits under each. Every channel leads with the top and goes as far down as it has room for. For the helmet, the core message might be "the helmet for riding all year", supported by seen on dark evenings, cool on long climbs and comfortable all day.

## How does the benefits ladder work?

The ladder climbs each message from the feature, to the practical benefit, to the emotional reason it matters, which is where Godin's idea shows up in copy. It draws on laddering, an interview method Thomas Reynolds and Jonathan Gutman set out in 1988 and that [researchers still describe](https://nsuworks.nova.edu/tqr/vol11/iss4/1) as a way to understand how consumers translate product attributes into meaningful associations with respect to self.

A line you can't climb down from doesn't belong in the launch.</li></ul></div></div></figure>

| Feature | Benefit | Emotional resonance |
|---|---|---|
| Integrated rear light | Drivers see you on dark evenings | You get home, and nobody at home has to worry |
| Larger vents | Cooler on long climbs | You ride further and feel strong doing it |
| Adjustable fit | Comfortable on a five-hour ride | You forget you're wearing it |

## Where should the helmet be seen?

Channels come next. Forrester's [No Media Should Stand Alone](https://www.forrester.com/report/No-Media-Should-Stand-Alone/RES54869) set out the roles of owned, earned and paid media in 2009 and advised using paid media as a catalyst around long-term owned touchpoints. For the helmet, the product page and email list sit at the centre, paid media sends the right riders there, and reviews and press make them believe it. The Basics guides to [owned, paid and earned media](/blog/owned-paid-earned-media-basics/) and [above and below the line](/blog/above-the-line-below-the-line-basics/) cover both ways of sorting channels.

The triggers set the timing. A crash replacement starts with a search, a new bike happens in a shop, and the clocks going back is a date in the plan. Watch the balance too. Les Binet and Peter Field's IPA report [The Long and the Short of It](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-balancing-short-and-long-term-marketing-strategies) warns that relying on very short-term online metrics has dangerous implications for long-term success, and a rider who doesn't ride in winter yet isn't searching for anything.

## Campaign, creative and PR

The launch campaign is a time-bound push with its own objective, budget and dates, carrying part of the strategy. The creative idea usually comes from the top rung of the ladder. PR and reviews earn the credibility paid media can't buy, and for a helmet, getting it to trusted reviewers early is part of the plan. The guides to [strategy, campaign, creative and execution](/blog/strategy-campaign-creative-execution-basics/) and [PR 101](/blog/pr-101-basics/) go deeper.

## Where SOSTAC brings it together

<figure class="dg dg-sostac-fig"><div class="dg-head"><span class="dg-kicker">Bringing it together · PR Smith's SOSTAC</span><span class="dg-title">The plan is a loop</span></div><ol class="dg-list dg-sostac"><li class="dg-stage dg-card dg-ev"><span class="dg-letter">S</span><span class="dg-stage-body"><span><span class="dg-strong">Situation</span><span class="dg-q">Where are we now?</span></span><span>Product-market fit and the research findings</span></span></li><li class="dg-stage dg-card"><span class="dg-letter">O</span><span class="dg-stage-body"><span><span class="dg-strong">Objectives</span><span class="dg-q">Where do we want to be?</span></span><span>Sales or new customers from the launch, by a date</span></span></li><li class="dg-stage dg-card"><span class="dg-letter">S</span><span class="dg-stage-body"><span><span class="dg-strong">Strategy</span><span class="dg-q">How do we get there?</span></span><span>Year-round riders, the position, the hierarchy</span></span></li><li class="dg-stage dg-card"><span class="dg-letter">T</span><span class="dg-stage-body"><span><span class="dg-strong">Tactics</span><span class="dg-q">The details of strategy</span></span><span>Price, stockists, channel plan, campaign, PR</span></span></li><li class="dg-stage dg-card"><span class="dg-letter">A</span><span class="dg-stage-body"><span><span class="dg-strong">Action</span><span class="dg-q">The details of tactics</span></span><span>Launch calendar, owners, budgets, briefs</span></span></li><li class="dg-stage dg-card dg-ev"><span class="dg-letter">C</span><span class="dg-stage-body"><span><span class="dg-strong">Control</span><span class="dg-q">How do we know we're getting there?</span></span><span>Who is buying, message playback, sales, margin</span></span></li></ol><div class="dg-note">↻ Control feeds the next Situation, so the plan is a loop rather than a document.</div></figure>

[PR Smith's SOSTAC](https://prsmith.org/sostac/) turns the chain into a plan. Fit and research are the Situation, launch targets are the Objectives, and the audience, position and hierarchy are the Strategy. The channel plan, campaign, creative and PR are Tactics, the launch calendar is Action, and Control checks who is buying, whether people can play back the core message, and sales and margin.

## Where does AI fit?

Each step has work AI can take on, from summarising reviews in research, to drafting ladders, to matching the story to the journalists who cover it. The [three layers of AI](/blog/three-layers-of-ai-basics/) guide explains which kind of AI suits which job. Whether there is a market, which finding matters and what the core message should be stay as human judgements.

Our view at Manual Focus is that AI makes this chain cheaper to run properly, so the research at the start and the measurement at the end, the steps teams used to skip, now deserve more attention. The order of the chain stays the same.
