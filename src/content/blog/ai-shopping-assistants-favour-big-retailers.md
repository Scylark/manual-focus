AI shopping assistants recommend national chains over smaller retailers by a wide margin, according to a study Lightspeed Commerce published on 29 September 2026. In head-to-head comparisons, ChatGPT and Google's AI tools picked the larger retailer up to 94% of the time, so specialist and challenger brands need to check how often assistants name them.

## Key takeaways

- Vaer AI ran about 20,000 shopping prompts through ChatGPT and Google's AI search for Lightspeed between June and August 2026, producing roughly 460,000 responses.
- Answering from training data alone, the assistants chose a national chain over a smaller retailer 90 to 94% of the time in head-to-head comparisons.
- With live web search switched on, national chains still took 46 to 58% of recommendations.
- Google AI Overviews recommended no local store in 68% of shopping answers, while ChatGPT and AI Mode named a local retailer in roughly 70% of theirs.
- Adding the word "independent" to a prompt cut the share of recommendations going to large chains from about 44% to as little as 9%.

**Update, 11 November 2026.** OpenAI now gives retailers a direct way to hand ChatGPT their product facts. On 10 November it launched [Businesses in ChatGPT](https://openai.com/index/introducing-businesses-chatgpt/) in beta, where an eligible business in a market with ChatGPT can claim its profile and keep its details current. Retailers can [upload product feeds](https://openai.com/business/businesses-in-chatgpt/) with pricing and availability. Business Hub also reports how often ChatGPT mentions the business, once there are enough conversations to meet OpenAI's privacy thresholds. OpenAI says claiming "does not guarantee higher placement or sales", so the study's advice on attributes and product-level tests still applies. For UK figures on which brands AI engines name, see [Manual Focus Light Meter](/blog/manual-focus-light-meter-ai-visibility/).

## What did the Lightspeed study find?

AI search consultancy Vaer AI ran the research for Lightspeed, which sells point-of-sale and payments software to retailers. According to [Lightspeed's release](https://www.lightspeedhq.com/news/ai-favors-big-retailers-over-local-stores-9-out-of-10-times-new-study-finds/), the team ran 20,000 shopping prompts across 10 product categories between June and August 2026, set in four North American cities including New York City and Montreal. It tested ChatGPT and two Google tools, AI Mode and AI Overviews. That produced roughly 460,000 responses and more than 2.4 million links. No prompt mentioned store size, so any lean toward big names came from the assistants themselves.

In head-to-head comparisons, answering from training data alone, the assistants chose the national chain over a smaller rival 90 to 94% of the time. Live web search narrowed the gap without closing it, since chains still took 46 to 58% of recommendations with search switched on. Local retailers were the top pick in roughly a third of broad category searches, falling to as little as one in ten once the shopper named a specific product. Google AI Overviews leaned hardest, recommending no local store in 68% of shopping answers, while ChatGPT and AI Mode named a local retailer in roughly 70% of theirs.

When Vaer AI re-ran matched prompts with "independent" added, the share of recommendations going to large chains dropped from about 44% to as little as 9%. A companion Censuswide survey of 2,000 North American consumers found 56% had used AI to help with a shopping decision, while 50% said they would shop locally more often if AI made independent retailers easier to find.

## Why do smaller brands lose out?

Smaller retailers lost most ground on the queries closest to a sale, since a shopper asking where to buy a particular model of trail shoe has usually decided what they want. A chain with national stock and years of reviews is likely to look like the safe answer to a model trained on the open web. Vaer AI's founder Tom Wells said in the release that the bias "begins in training".

Live search helped smaller retailers because the assistant reads current pages and listings instead of relying on training data. Content and SEO teams can only influence that half, so they should concentrate on the pages assistants fetch, such as product pages and the editorial round-ups beside them.

Because assistants changed their answers on a single word in the prompt, shoppers' own descriptions of what they want shape which retailers get considered. That makes it a [category entry point](/lens/demand/category-entry-points/) problem. If your customers choose you because you are independent or specialist, that attribute has to appear in plain text wherever an assistant reads about you.

Our view at Manual Focus is that challenger retailers and DTC brands should stop treating AI visibility as a single score and track it at product level, because the study shows a brand can look healthy on broad category prompts and still disappear at the point of purchase. Retailers already watching AI referral traffic for peak trading, as we set out in [our read of Adobe's holiday forecast](/blog/adobe-ai-shopping-forecast-black-friday/), can add this as the visibility measure that sits upstream of those clicks.

## Which findings matter most?

Each finding points to a different fix.

| Finding | What a smaller brand should do |
|---|---|
| Chains win 90 to 94% on training data alone | Put your effort into live search answers |
| Chains still take 46 to 58% with live search | Fix the product and stockist pages assistants fetch |
| Local picks fall to one in ten on product queries | Track AI visibility for each product |
| "Independent" cuts the chain share to 9% | State that attribute in plain text everywhere |

## How can a small brand get named?

1. **Write 30 product-level prompts.** Use your ten best sellers and phrase three buying questions for each the way a customer would, including your town or region where location matters.
2. **Run them in pairs.** Ask each prompt plain, then again with the attribute that sets you apart, such as "independent" or "specialist", in ChatGPT and both Google AI tools. Record who gets named in each version.
3. **Put the attribute where assistants read.** Add it in plain words to your product pages and product feeds, rather than only in a logo or image.
4. **Find the pages assistants cite.** Note which round-ups and review sites appear in the links for the prompts you lose, then brief your PR team or agency on getting listed in them.
5. **Repeat monthly into peak.** Re-run the same set in early November to see what moved before Black Friday.

## What are the study's limits?

Lightspeed commissioned the study and sells software to the smaller retailers it describes, so it has a commercial interest in the finding. It also used the release to launch an AI visibility campaign aimed at them. The release gives headline figures and a methodology note but no full dataset or category breakdown. Vaer AI used an AI classifier to sort each retailer by size. The release's opening also names Gemini among the systems analysed, though its methodology leaves Gemini out. The prompts were set in four North American cities and the survey covers North American shoppers only, so there is no UK figure yet. The results also reflect the versions of each assistant that were live between June and August, before OpenAI put [GPT-6 into ChatGPT](/blog/chatgpt-gpt-6-intelligent-ui-marketing-teams/) on 7 October. None of it explains why a given brand was left out, which only your own prompt tests will show.
