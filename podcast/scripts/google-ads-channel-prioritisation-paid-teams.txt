JAMES: Google Ads has just given paid teams a dial where there used to be a switch. On the fourteenth of October it announced two settings that let you rank channels like Display and search partners, and cap what the low priority ones can spend. You're listening to Manual Focus. I'm James Vickers, and Hannah, who looks after the AI side here, is with me.

HANNAH: Hi James. And there's a deadline in this one. Search campaigns that run on Display move to the new system in February twenty twenty-seven, whether you touch them or not.

JAMES: Start with what Google actually announced.

HANNAH: We read Google's blog post, by Tal Akabas, a director of product management at Google Ads, and the help page. The first setting is channel prioritisation. You mark a channel, say search partners or the Google Display Network, as high or low priority. Google AI spends on high priority channels first, up to your Target CPA or Target ROAS, so your cost per acquisition or return on ad spend goal, and only then sends what's left to the low priority ones. Everything starts as high priority.

JAMES: And the second?

HANNAH: Budget allocation. For a low priority channel you set a percentage of the campaign's average daily budget as a ceiling. Google's example is five per cent of a thousand-dollar daily budget, which aims to keep that channel at fifty dollars a day or less. It resets daily, and zero per cent stops spend on that channel.

JAMES: [dryly] "Aims to" is doing some work there.

HANNAH: It is. Google calls it best-effort, so a channel can go over on a given day, and the help page doesn't say by how much.

JAMES: Which campaigns get this?

HANNAH: Search, Shopping, Performance Max and Demand Gen, rolling out over the coming months, with Demand Gen later. In Performance Max and Demand Gen, Search, YouTube, Maps, Gmail and Discover count as one block called Google channels. You can push Display below that block, but you can't rank YouTube above Search. And Demand Gen gets prioritisation without the budget cap.

JAMES: Here's what a head of paid search will ask first. Does ranking a channel low actually change where the money goes?

HANNAH: Only if the campaign runs out of budget. Google's help page says prioritisation has no effect when a campaign isn't budget-limited, because spare budget funds every channel that converts at the target anyway. The cap still applies, though, and Google's blog says it matters most for Target CPA and Target ROAS campaigns with headroom.

JAMES: So on a budget-limited campaign, ranking search partners low protects your Google Search volume. On a campaign with headroom, only the percentage cap moves money.

HANNAH: And look at who Google's talking to. The announcement speaks directly to marketers who have, in its words, historically opted out of search partners and Display.

JAMES: [thoughtfully] Our view at Manual Focus is that most brands should swap those opt-outs for low priority and a small cap. An opt-out produces no evidence. A five per cent cap gives you a test you can read after a month.

HANNAH: What does that look like in practice?

JAMES: Take a cycling or endurance retailer. Search budgets are tightest before Black Friday. Set Display or search partners to low priority at five per cent on a budget-limited bikes or kit campaign in late October, and you've got four weeks of evidence before peak spend. If it earns its place, raise the cap for the sales. If it doesn't, you've lost very little.

HANNAH: Now, February.

JAMES: Go on.

HANNAH: From February twenty twenty-seven, every Search campaign with Display Expansion on migrates automatically, and Google says the current setup will go away. Spend may shift between channels. To keep today's behaviour, Google's advice is Display at low priority with a fifty per cent allocation. And in the same paragraph of the help page, Display stops serving on Search campaigns that use Target Impression Share.

JAMES: So brand campaigns built on impression share lose Display whatever you choose. Anything else for agencies?

HANNAH: You can't set any of this from a manager account, and Google Ads API and Editor support is still to come. It's account by account.

JAMES: Right, so rather than a list of chores, get AI on this. First, get AI on finding every Search campaign that migrates.

HANNAH: Give it a campaign export with Display status, bid strategy and budget status, and it hands back a table: which campaigns move in February, which use Target Impression Share, and which are budget-limited, so you know where prioritisation bites.

JAMES: Second, get your AI to read the capped test.

HANNAH: Set it up as a weekly routine from the day the test starts. Feed it the campaign results, and at four weeks it judges the test on cost per acquisition and new customer share, and says plainly what it couldn't check, because the help page doesn't describe channel-level reporting.

JAMES: Third, get AI on turning the caps into a budget sheet, account by account.

HANNAH: Give it your account list and your quarterly mix, and it drafts a row per account and campaign, with the proposed priority, the percentage and a dated note for each change. Choosing the numbers, and signing in to apply them, stays with your team.

JAMES: The prompts are in the show notes. So what don't we know yet?

HANNAH: [thoughtfully] How far over the cap spend can go. Whether the reporting will be good enough to judge a test. Whether a low priority channel gets worse inventory or just less budget. And Google gave no performance data. It says the controls help marketers spend confidently as the holidays approach, which I'd read as a reason to test before peak, not proof that they work.

JAMES: So the takeaway is this. Google AI still sets the bids, but you now choose the order it spends in and the limit on each channel, and every Search campaign on Display needs a deliberate setting before February.
