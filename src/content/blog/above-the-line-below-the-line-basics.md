Above-the-line marketing is advertising in mass media, such as television, radio, press and outdoor, that reaches a whole market at once. Below-the-line marketing reaches people more directly, through direct mail, email, events, sampling and point-of-sale. Through-the-line campaigns combine the two under one idea, which is how most campaigns are planned today.

## Key takeaways

- Above the line means mass media aimed at a broad audience, used mainly to build awareness and memory of a brand.
- Below the line means direct, targeted activity such as direct mail, trade shows, events and promotions, usually aimed at people closer to buying.
- Through the line means running both under one campaign idea, so each makes the other work harder.
- Digital channels sit on both sides. Broad online video behaves like above the line, while search and retargeting behave like below the line.
- The IPA's Binet and Field report warned against judging marketing mainly on short-term online metrics, which is the trap a below-the-line-only plan falls into.

## Where do the terms come from?

The usual explanation is a line in a marketing budget, with mass-media advertising above it and other promotional costs below it. The [Cambridge Business English Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/above-the-line) now defines above-the-line as relating to advertising on television, in newspapers, on the internet and similar media, rather than other ways of telling people about a company. Its [below-the-line entry](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/below-the-line) covers work such as trade shows and direct mail, which communicate more directly with customers and cost less than television and newspaper advertising.

You will often read that the terms started at a particular company in a particular year. We couldn't find a primary record of that story, so we have left it out.

## What counts as above the line?

Above-the-line, or ATL, is any media that reaches a broad audience you don't select individually. Television, radio, national and regional press, outdoor posters, cinema and broad-reach online video all count. Its job is mainly to build memory, so that when someone in the market thinks of the category, your brand comes to mind.

ATL is hard to measure in short-term response, because most of the people who see it aren't buying that week. That is the point of it rather than a flaw. The [Category Entry Point research](/lens/demand/category-entry-points/) playbook helps decide which buying moments your mass media should link to the brand.

## What counts as below the line?

Below-the-line, or BTL, reaches a narrower group more directly. Direct mail, email, events, trade shows, sampling, sales promotions, point-of-sale displays in shops, and the activation around a sponsorship all sit here. BTL usually targets people close to buying, so its results show up quickly and are easier to count.

The [event sponsorship](/lens/demand/event-sponsorship-playbook/) and [retail partner programme](/lens/demand/retail-partner-programme/) playbooks are both largely below-the-line work, and the [lifecycle journey builder](/lens/demand/lifecycle-journey-builder/) covers the email and CRM side.

| | Above the line | Below the line | Through the line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audience | Broad, not individually selected | Narrow, often named or segmented | Both, under one idea |
| Main job | Build awareness and memory | Prompt action from people near buying | Join memory to action |
| Examples | TV, radio, press, outdoor, cinema, broad online video | Direct mail, email, events, sampling, point-of-sale, promotions | A TV launch with matching retail displays, email and events |
| How it's measured | Reach, brand awareness, long-term sales | Response, redemptions, leads, short-term sales | Both, reviewed together |
| Typical risk | Spend with no visible short-term return | Squeezing existing demand without creating more | Two teams running two ideas |

## What does through the line mean?

Through-the-line, or TTL, is a campaign that uses above-the-line and below-the-line activity together under one idea. A launch film builds awareness, retail displays and sampling meet people in the shop, and an email sequence follows up with those who signed up. Each piece is more recognisable because of the others.

For most marketing teams today TTL is simply what good campaign planning looks like, and the useful lesson is to give each line a clear job rather than to argue about which line matters more. The Basics piece on [strategy, campaigns, creative and execution](/blog/strategy-campaign-creative-execution-basics/) covers how one creative idea carries across a campaign.

## Is digital above or below the line?

Digital is on both sides, which is why the old line has blurred. A homepage takeover on a national news site or a broad online video buy works like ATL. A search ad, a retargeting ad or a personalised email works like BTL. Paid social can be either, depending on how tightly it is targeted.

The blur matters because digital made BTL-style measurement available for everything, and it is tempting to judge all media on short-term clicks. Les Binet and Peter Field's IPA report [The Long and the Short of It](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-balancing-short-and-long-term-marketing-strategies) examined how campaign effects build over time and warned that relying on short-term online metrics as the main measures could harm long-term success.

Our view at Manual Focus is that the line is still a useful planning question even if the media have changed, because asking "is this building memory or prompting action" stops a team pouring its whole budget into harvesting demand it never created. The [channel-mix simulator](/lens/demand/channel-mix-simulator/) helps model that split before the money is committed, and [paid channels in the AI era](/blog/paid-channels-bidding-agents/) covers how platform bidding agents now run much of the targeted side.

## How ATL and BTL map to paid, owned and earned

ATL and BTL describe reach and purpose. Paid, owned and earned describe who controls the channel. Almost all ATL is paid media, while BTL mixes paid activity such as promotions with owned channels such as your email list. The two frames answer different questions, and [owned, paid and earned media explained](/blog/owned-paid-earned-media-basics/) covers the second one.
