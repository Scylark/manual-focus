SOSTAC is a six-stage marketing planning model created by the UK marketer PR Smith in the 1990s. The letters stand for Situation, Objectives, Strategy, Tactics, Action and Control. Each stage answers one question, from where are we now to how do we know we are getting there, so a team can plan a campaign or a year the same way.

## Key takeaways

- SOSTAC stands for Situation, Objectives, Strategy, Tactics, Action and Control, and was created by PR Smith in the 1990s.
- The model separates strategy, the route to the objectives, from tactics, the marketing mix that carries it out.
- Situation and Control are the evidence stages, and most weak plans are thin on both.
- PR Smith wrote for the Chartered Institute of Marketing in September 2025 that any AI-generated plan should be treated as a first draft only.
- The same six stages work for a one-page campaign plan and for an annual marketing plan.

## What is SOSTAC?

SOSTAC is a planning checklist in the order you should think. PR Smith's [own summary](https://prsmith.org/sostac/) gives each stage a question, and the model is popular because those questions stop a team jumping straight to tactics, which is where most marketing conversations start. The digital marketing author Dave Chaffey [notes](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/sostac-marketing-plan-model) that it was rated among the most popular marketing models in a poll of marketers run by the Chartered Institute of Marketing.

| Stage | Question it answers | What goes in it | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation | Where are we now? | Customers, competitors, performance, strengths and weaknesses | A data dump with no conclusions |
| Objectives | Where do we want to be? | Measurable goals with dates, tied to business results | Activity goals such as "post three times a week" |
| Strategy | How do we get there? | Target audience, positioning, channel choices, partners | A list of tactics called a strategy |
| Tactics | What exactly will we do? | The marketing mix, campaigns, content, pricing, channels | Tactics that don't trace back to the strategy |
| Action | Who does what, when? | Owners, timelines, budgets, briefs, processes | No named owner for each piece |
| Control | Are we getting there? | Measures, reporting rhythm, review points | Measuring what's easy instead of what the objective needs |

## Where are we now?

Situation analysis is the evidence base for everything after it. It covers your customers and what they want, your competitors and how they position themselves, your own results against last year's objectives, and the strengths and weaknesses that matter to the next twelve months.

The test of a good situation analysis is that it ends in conclusions. Five pages of charts with no "so what" leave the team to guess which facts matter. Three or four clear findings, each with its evidence, give the objectives something to stand on. The [positioning audit pipeline](/lens/brand/positioning-audit-pipeline/) and [Category Entry Point research](/lens/demand/category-entry-points/) playbooks both produce situation evidence a team can plan from.

## How do objectives differ from goals?

In SOSTAC the objectives say where you want to be, and PR Smith recommends SMART objectives, specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. The useful discipline is to tie them to business results. "Grow online revenue from new customers by 15% by next September" gives the plan a target. "Increase brand awareness" doesn't, because nobody can tell whether it was met.

Good plans also separate the business objective from the marketing objectives that serve it. Revenue is the business objective. New customer numbers, conversion rate or repeat purchase rate are marketing objectives that drive it.

## How do strategy and tactics differ?

Strategy is the route. It says who you will target, how you will position the brand against competitors, which channels carry the plan and which partners you will work with. Tactics are the details of that route, the marketing mix of specific products, prices, places and promotions.

The two blur in most plans, and the cost is real. A list of channels and campaigns labelled "strategy" gives no basis for saying no to anything. One way to check is to ask whether the item would still be true if every campaign changed. "Win cyclists who train indoors through the winter, through coaches and clubs rather than paid social" survives a change of campaign, so it is strategy. "Run a January Strava challenge" is a tactic that serves it.

## What goes in Action and Control?

Action turns tactics into work. It names an owner for each piece, sets the timeline, allocates the budget and covers the processes and briefs that get work out of the door. PR Smith's description of the stage centres on making sure the tactics are carried out well, which in practice means the boring operational detail most plans skip.

Control says how you will know whether it's working. It sets the measures for each objective, the reporting rhythm and the review points where the team decides what to change. A plan without Control can't learn, so the same mistakes reappear the following year. The [attribution tear-down](/lens/demand/attribution-teardown/) playbook helps here, because it tests whether the numbers in your dashboard reflect what actually drove results.

## How does AI change SOSTAC?

PR Smith addressed this himself in a [September 2025 article for the CIM](https://www.cim.co.uk/content-insights/articles/sostac-plans-in-the-ai-age/), alongside an AI edition of his SOSTAC guide. He named situation analysis, strategy, tactics and actions as stages where AI is especially relevant, and advised treating any AI-generated plan as a first draft only.

Mapped to the [three layers of AI](/blog/three-layers-of-ai-basics/), the split looks like this.

| Stage | Where AI helps | What stays with a person |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Pulling and summarising data, competitor reviews, research synthesis | Deciding which findings matter |
| Objectives | Modelling what different targets would require | Choosing the targets and owning them |
| Strategy | Stress-testing options and spotting gaps | The choice of audience and position |
| Tactics | Drafting campaign ideas, content plans and briefs | Picking which ideas to back |
| Action | Building timelines, briefs and recurring reports | Accountability for delivery |
| Control | Weekly reporting and anomaly checks | Deciding what to change |

Our view at Manual Focus is that AI makes SOSTAC more useful, not less, because it removes the hours that once made a thorough situation analysis and a proper control loop too expensive for most teams. The [quarterly planning ritual](/lens/ops/quarterly-planning-ritual/) playbook shows how to run that loop every quarter rather than once a year.

## How to use SOSTAC on one page

For a single campaign, give each stage two or three lines. State the situation finding the campaign responds to, the one objective it serves, the audience and position, the main activity, the owner and date, and the single number you will judge it by. If any stage is blank, the campaign isn't ready to brief.
