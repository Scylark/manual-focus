HANNAH: It's a prediction engine for text. Anthropic's own glossary puts it plainly: in pretraining, the model learns to predict the next word from the text that comes before it, across an enormous amount of written material. On its own, that raw predictor isn't much of an assistant. So there's further training, including people ranking answers against each other, and that's what turns it into something that follows instructions and holds a conversation.

JAMES: So when it writes a headline for me, it's predicting what a good headline probably looks like.

HANNAH: Given everything in front of it, yes. And "everything in front of it" is the important bit. There's a thing called the context window, which is the model's working memory for the conversation. It's the text it can look back at while it answers. If your brand guidelines, your audience research and your product facts aren't in that window, the model falls back on general patterns.

JAMES: [dryly] Which is why a generic prompt gets you generic copy.

HANNAH: Exactly that. The second property is that because it's predicting, it can be confidently wrong. A group of OpenAI researchers published a paper in September twenty twenty-five arguing that models make things up partly because training and testing reward a good guess over saying "I don't know". So any fact, figure or quote that comes out of a chat window needs checking at the source before it goes anywhere.

JAMES: And the third thing, the one I'd underline for a marketing lead, is who carries the work at layer one.

HANNAH: The person does. You paste in the brief, you read the draft, you ask for changes, you copy the result into your content system. The model never touches your systems. It only sees what you show it.

HANNAH: You stop asking for answers and start asking for outcomes. An agent is a language model that's been given tools, such as web search, a browser, access to files, or a connection to your analytics, and the freedom to decide which tool to use and when. Then it checks its own progress and keeps going until it thinks the job's done.

JAMES: There's a distinction in the Anthropic guide on this that I found useful.

HANNAH: Their December twenty twenty-four piece, Building effective agents. They separate workflows, where the model and its tools follow steps someone has laid down in advance, from agents, where the model directs its own process and decides which tools to use. They call both of them agentic systems. And their advice is to find the simplest thing that works and only add complexity when you need it.

JAMES: So give me the marketing version of that difference.

HANNAH: "Write me three subject lines" is a layer one request. "Pull last month's email results, find the three weakest flows, work out why from the content and the send times, and give me a one-page brief to fix them" is a layer two request. The agent opens the export, does the analysis, and comes back with the brief.

JAMES: [thoughtfully] And that second request is the one that used to take someone a morning. What does it need to work?

Second, a clear definition of done. An agent stops when it thinks the job is finished. If you haven't said what finished looks like, which data to trust and where to stop and ask, it decides for itself.

JAMES: So it's expensive, and it's for work that's worth it.

JAMES: In a marketing team, layer three looks like a pipeline. HANNAH: Teams that skip layer one end up automating inconsistent prompts. Teams that skip layer two end up orchestrating agents that were never reliable on their own.
