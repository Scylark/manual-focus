James and Hannah talk through why ranking on Google no longer predicts who
gets quoted in AI answers. A Brandlight analysis published by 5W in May 2026
found the overlap between top organic Google links and AI-cited sources fell
from around 70% in early 2024 to below 20% by April 2026. They cover why
models pick sources on different signals from a ranked list, the four things
that earn a citation (answering first, original data, checkable claims and
third-party corroboration), why smaller teams still have a first-mover
window, and three jobs to hand to AI.

## Get AI on this

Paste these into whichever AI assistant you use: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or
Copilot. Swap the bracketed parts for your own details. The first two need an
assistant that can open web pages, or you can paste the page text in. The
third needs one that can browse the web and run on a monthly schedule.

### 1. Check your opening paragraphs

```text
I run marketing for [brand], [one line on what you sell] at [website].

AI answer engines are more likely to quote a page that fully answers its main
question in the first 200 words, rather than warming up to it. I want to know
which of our key pages do that.

Here are the pages and the main question each one should answer:
[URL] | [question]
[URL] | [question]
...

For each page, read the first 200 words and give me a table with the columns:
URL | Main question | Answered in the first 200 words? (fully/partly/no) |
Where the answer actually appears (first paragraph, later, nowhere) | Words
before the answer starts.

Then, for every page marked "partly" or "no", draft a new opening of under
200 words that answers the question directly, using only facts already on
the page. If you couldn't open a page, or it's behind a login, say so in that
row rather than guessing.
```

### 2. Find the vague claims

```text
I run marketing for [brand] at [website]. AI models skip vague superlatives
and prefer specific, falsifiable, sourced claims they can stand behind. I want
to find the vague claims on our key pages.

Pages to check: [paste URLs, or paste the page text].

List every superlative or vague claim (for example "best", "leading",
"unrivalled", "trusted by thousands"). Give me a table with the columns:
URL | Claim as written | Why a model might skip it | A specific, checkable
version | Evidence needed to support it.

Don't invent figures, test results or awards for the checkable version. Use
[brackets] where we need to supply a real number or source, and flag any
claim where you can't see what evidence would back it up.
```

### 3. Check who gets cited every month

```text
Set this up as a routine that runs on the first Monday of each month.

I run marketing for [brand] at [website]. Our main competitors are
[competitors]. These are the questions our customers ask before buying:
[paste 10 to 20 questions].

For each question, ask it in the AI answer tools you can reach, including
Google's AI Overviews where available, and record which sources
the answer cites.

Give me a table with the columns: Date | Question | Tool | Sources cited (in
order) | Are we cited? (yes/no, and position) | Competitor cited instead.

Compare it with last month's run and list what changed. If a tool showed no
AI answer, wouldn't load, or didn't show its sources, say so in that row
rather than leaving it blank.
```

### And one for you

The original data. A survey you ran or a benchmark you measured gives the
model a number it can only get from you. AI can help analyse the results,
but deciding what's worth measuring and running it is a job for your team.
