If you've joined a marketing team in the last ten years, there's a fair chance someone used those phrases in your first week and nobody explained them. They're older than digital marketing, and they're still a useful way to think about a budget. I'm James Vickers, this is Manual Focus, and this Basics episode is the plain-English version, with Hannah.

HANNAH: Hi James. I'm glad we're doing this one, because I'll be honest, the first time I heard "below the line" I assumed it meant something slightly disreputable.

JAMES: [chuckles] It doesn't, though it's never sounded glamorous. Start with the definitions.

HANNAH: Above the line, or ATL, is advertising in mass media that reaches a whole market at once. Television, radio, newspapers, outdoor posters, cinema, and these days broad-reach online video. Below the line, or BTL, reaches people more directly and selectively. Direct mail, email, events, trade shows, sampling, sales promotions, displays in shops. And through the line, TTL, is a campaign that uses both together under one idea.

JAMES: And where do the names come from? People always ask.

HANNAH: The usual explanation is a literal line in a marketing budget. Mass-media advertising sat above it, and the other promotional costs went below it. The Cambridge Business English Dictionary now defines above-the-line as advertising on television, in newspapers, on the internet and so on, rather than other ways of telling people about a company. Its below-the-line entry covers things like trade shows and direct mail, which communicate more directly with customers and cost less than television and newspaper advertising.

JAMES: You'll also read that the terms started at one particular company in one particular year.

HANNAH: You will, and we went looking for a primary record of that story and couldn't find one. So it's not in the article, and I'm not going to repeat it here.

JAMES: Good. So let's take them one at a time. Above the line. What's it for?

HANNAH: Mainly memory. Its job is that when someone in the market thinks of the category, your brand comes to mind. Most of the people who see a TV ad aren't buying that week, so it's hard to measure in short-term response.

JAMES: And that's the bit finance teams struggle with. You'll usually see the conversation go like this. The response numbers on the TV campaign look poor, so someone proposes moving the money into search, where the numbers look great. And it works, for a while.

HANNAH: Until?

JAMES: Until the search volume dries up, because nobody's building the demand that search was harvesting. That's the point of mass media rather than a flaw in it. The Category Entry Point research playbook in The Lens helps you decide which buying moments your mass media should link the brand to, so it's doing a specific job, not just being seen.

HANNAH: Then below the line. What's that for?

JAMES: Prompting action from people who are close to buying. Direct mail, email, events, trade shows, sampling, promotions, displays in shops, the activation around a sponsorship. Its results show up quickly and they're easy to count. Leads, redemptions, footfall, sales that week.

HANNAH: And the risk with BTL on its own?

JAMES: You squeeze existing demand without creating any more. It looks efficient, every pound is measured, and the market you're selling to slowly gets smaller. The event sponsorship and retail partner programme playbooks are both largely below-the-line work, and the lifecycle journey builder covers the email and CRM side.

HANNAH: Does any of this apply in business-to-business marketing? It sounds very consumer.

JAMES: It applies, the mix just looks different. In most business-to-business teams, below the line is the bulk of the budget. Trade shows, events, direct outreach, email, the sales team's own materials. Above the line is often small or missing altogether, and that's usually the gap. If nobody in the market has heard of you before your salesperson calls, every deal starts from cold.

HANNAH: So the same memory and action split, just weighted the other way. Which is interesting, because I'd have guessed the jargon belonged to consumer brands and big television budgets.

JAMES: It's a fair guess, and a common one, but the idea travels well beyond that. And for a business-to-business team, above the line might well be broad-reach online video or a sponsorship rather than television, which is where the digital blur comes in. We'll get to that.

HANNAH: Which brings us to through the line.

JAMES: TTL is simply what good campaign planning looks like for most teams now. One idea, carried across both. A launch film builds awareness, retail displays and sampling meet people in the shop, and an email sequence follows up with those who signed up. Each piece is more recognisable because of the others.

HANNAH: So the useful thing isn't arguing which line matters more.

JAMES: No, it's giving each line a clear job. Memory or action. And where I'd say it still goes wrong in a lot of businesses is structural. Two teams, sometimes two agencies, one doing brand and one doing response, running two different ideas at the same audience. The customer doesn't know or care which budget paid for what, they just see a brand that seems to say two things.

HANNAH: Our strategy and campaigns Basics episode made the same point about one creative idea running through a whole campaign.

JAMES: So if you're planning a through-the-line campaign from scratch, there are four steps in the article. Write the objective and the audience once, so every channel, mass or targeted, works to the same brief. Give each line a job, memory for the mass work and action for the targeted work. Keep one idea running through both. And measure both horizons, short-term response from the targeted pieces, longer-term brand measures from the mass pieces, and review them side by side.

HANNAH: The last one sounds like the hard part.

JAMES: It is, because the two sets of numbers move at different speeds. The response numbers arrive in days, the brand numbers in months. If you review them separately, the fast numbers win every argument.
