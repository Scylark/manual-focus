Across 100 websites, Marie Haynes found ChatGPT delivered 0.62% as many sessions as Google organic search, in a study published on 21 October 2026. Two more studies that week, from The SEO Works and Kevin Indig, pointed the same way, so marketing leaders should keep AI search budgets modest and stop judging them on referral clicks.

## Key takeaways

- Marie Haynes counted ChatGPT sessions at 0.62% of Google organic volume across 100 websites and over 145 million GA4 sessions, rising to 0.73% with every other AI assistant added.
- The SEO Works counted 72,095 AI assistant sessions against 2,825,594 organic sessions across 95 sites between September 2025 and August 2026, so organic sent 39 times more traffic.
- AI visitors converted better than organic visitors in both studies, at 13.8% against 3.02% in Haynes's data and 7.18% against 6.08% in The SEO Works' data, but at 0.67 times the rate of paid search.
- Kevin Indig found on 19 October 2026 that direct sessions with identifiable AI traces made up 0.003% of direct traffic across 12 sites.
- The SEO Works left Google AI Overviews out of its count, because GA4 records their clicks as ordinary organic sessions.

## Three studies, one direction

Search Engine Roundtable [grouped the three together](https://www.seroundtable.com/ai-search-studies-42359.html) on 22 October. Each used a different sample and asked a slightly different question, which makes the agreement more useful than any one of them.

| Study | Published | Sample | AI traffic found | Conversions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marie Haynes, GEO is a distraction | 21 October 2026 | 100 sites, 12 months of GA4, over 145 million sessions | ChatGPT at 0.62% of Google organic sessions | ChatGPT 13.8% against Google organic 3.02%, with Google organic driving 27 times more conversions than all AI assistants combined |
| The SEO Works, AI referral traffic benchmark | Data to August 2026 | 95 GA4 properties, 63 in the conversion comparison | AI assistants at 2.6% of organic sessions | AI 7.18% against organic 6.08%, or 3.2 AI conversions for every 100 from organic |
| Kevin Indig, Growth Memo | 19 October 2026 | 12 sites, mostly B2B, August against September 2026 | AI traces in 0.003% of direct sessions | Not measured |

[Haynes](https://www.mariehaynes.com/geo-is-a-distraction/) also reports that ChatGPT's referral growth stopped in August 2025, around the GPT-5 release. [The SEO Works](https://www.seoworks.co.uk/downloads/ai-referral-traffic-benchmark/) found a gentler rise, with AI referrals climbing from 2.29% to 3.04% of organic sessions from May 2026. Both put AI assistants below about 3% of organic search sessions.

## Do AI visitors convert better?

Against organic search they mostly do, and the gap shrinks as the samples get larger. Haynes's ChatGPT visitors converted at more than four times the Google organic rate, but only 55 of her 100 sites tracked conversions at all. The SEO Works pooled 63 sites and found a 1.18 times advantage over organic, with AI converting worse than organic on 23 of them. AI traffic converted at 0.67 times the rate of paid search and at 2.48 times the rate of direct traffic.

The SEO Works also split its results by business type, putting B2C sites at 1.51 times the organic rate and B2B product sites at 0.62 times. Its conversions are GA4 key events, which the study treats as upper-bound counts, so a form view or a download can sit beside a sale.

A higher conversion rate on 2.6% of the traffic still adds up to a small number of customers, so for most brands the conversion story isn't enough to justify a large AI search budget on its own.

## Is GEO a distraction?

Haynes argues it is, for most sites, and recommends that teams stop prioritising ChatGPT citations and work on Google Search and Google's own AI features instead. Her data supports the first half. The second half needs a caveat that The SEO Works spells out, because clicks from Google AI Overviews arrive in GA4 as organic sessions. When AI Overviews appear on most of a brand's searches, as [DemandSphere's September data](/blog/google-ai-overviews-brand-searches/) suggested, some of what these studies call Google traffic already comes through an AI answer.

The studies also measure what happens after a click. An assistant that names your brand in an answer can shape a shortlist without sending anyone to your site, which is the argument we made in [the citation moved off the page](/blog/the-citation-moved-off-the-page/). Indig went looking for that effect in direct and branded traffic and couldn't find it reliably. Across his 12 sites total direct sessions rose 140.9% from August to September while engaged direct sessions fell 1.0%, and one site logged 409,000 direct sessions of which 198 were engaged. Teams that read rising direct traffic as an AI dividend are often counting bots.

Manual Focus sells AI visibility measurement through [Light Meter](/blog/manual-focus-light-meter-ai-visibility/), so weigh what follows with that in mind. Our view at Manual Focus is that these studies settle the referral question, since AI assistants are a small traffic source for most brands, and leave the influence question open, so AI search work should be sized to a modest share of the SEO budget and judged on how often buyers see your brand in AI answers and on what new customers say, with referral sessions as the third signal. Indig recommends the same three signals on one monthly report.

## What to change in your reporting

1. **Work out your own ratio.** In GA4, match session source against the pattern The SEO Works used, `bard|chatgpt|openai|claude|copilot|gemini|perplexity|grok`, and compare those sessions with organic search over 12 months. If you land near 2% to 3%, plan your budget around that figure.
2. **Clean direct traffic before anyone reads it.** Split direct sessions by engagement, landing page and browser, as Indig did, and look for spikes of unengaged visits before crediting AI with any growth.
3. **Ask new customers where they heard of you.** Add AI assistants as an option in the lead form or post-purchase survey. Indig cites Ramp, whose self-reported answers attributed 30% to 40% of its direct traffic to answer engines, a figure no analytics tool would have shown. Our [attribution tear-down playbook](/lens/demand/attribution-teardown/) covers how to weigh self-reported answers against platform data.
4. **Track exposure on a fixed prompt set.** Pick the questions your buyers ask, run them on the same engines each month and record whether your brand appears. Repeat each question several times, because single answers vary from run to run.
5. **Fund Google's AI surfaces first.** Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode sit inside the channel that still drives most of your traffic, and our post on [Google's new AI Mode links](/blog/google-ai-mode-official-websites-links/) covers how brand sites earn those clicks.

## What the studies can't tell you

Haynes calls hers "my small dataset" and doesn't say how the 100 sites were chosen beyond random sampling. The SEO Works gives customer types but not sectors or countries, and doesn't break results down by assistant. Indig's sample skews B2B and US, and his visibility tests rest on 17 and 35 usable weekly observations, which he notes limits their precision. None of the three measures AI Overviews or AI Mode clicks separately, because GA4 doesn't show them. The trend is also unsettled, with Haynes seeing ChatGPT referrals flat since August 2025 and The SEO Works seeing a rise since May 2026. Retail could look different in the holiday season, where [Adobe forecast AI referrals to US retail sites up 130%](/blog/adobe-ai-shopping-forecast-black-friday/) year on year over November and December.
