Before you start

About this book

Ask a room of people what marketing is and most of them will describe advertising. Advertising is the visible end of marketing, and it should be the last thing you decide.

This minibook joins up the ideas in the Manual Focus Basics series into one answer. It starts with two formal definitions and a softer one, then follows a single product, a new road helmet, from the first question a marketer should ask to the last number they should check.

Each chapter covers one step. Each step answers one question and hands its answer to the next. By the end you will have the whole chain, a planning model to run it with, and a clear view of where AI helps and where it doesn't.

The helmet is hypothetical

The helmet, the brand behind it and its research findings are invented to show how the steps connect. They are not a real launch, and nothing in this book reports real sales, prices or survey results. Where the book cites a fact, it names the source, and the sources are listed at the back.

How to use it

  • Read it straight through to see how the steps depend on each other.
  • Use the worksheets at the end of each chapter on a real product of your own.
  • Hand the AI prompts in chapter 10 to whichever assistant your company uses.

Chapter 01

What marketing is

There are two definitions most people are taught, and a softer one worth keeping beside them.

The management process responsible for identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer requirements profitably.Chartered Institute of Marketing

The Chartered Institute of Marketing, the professional body for marketers in the UK, uses this definition, and it is the one most UK courses teach. Every word in it does a job.

  • Identifying means finding out what people need now.
  • Anticipating means spotting what they will need next. Most teams react well to what customers said last year and leave little time to ask what will be different next year, which is usually where a competitor gets in.
  • Satisfying means meeting the need, which is a product and service question as much as a communication one.
  • Profitably is the word that gets forgotten in a launch, when everyone is excited about the product and nobody has checked the margin at the price the market will pay.

The institute goes on to spell out what marketing decides. It covers what a company will produce, what it will charge, how it will get the product or service to the customer, and how it will tell customers about it. It sums these up as the 7Ps.

The 7PsThe question it answers
ProductWhat will we make or offer?
PriceWhat will we charge?
PlaceHow and where will customers get it?
PromotionHow will we tell customers about it?
PeopleWho delivers it, and how do they behave?
ProcessHow does the customer move through buying and using it?
Physical evidenceWhat can the customer see and touch that proves it?

Promotion is one of seven. Most people meet marketing from the promotion end, through adverts, posts and emails, which is why so many marketing plans begin with channels. A price change or a new stockist can do more for a business objective than another campaign, and a better advert will not fix a problem whose cause is price.

The activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large.American Marketing Association

The American Marketing Association's definition is broader, and it takes in partners and society as well as customers. Both formal definitions start with the customer and treat telling people about the product as one part of the job among several.

The softer one

Seth Godin's book This Is Marketing, published by Portfolio in November 2018, offers a different emphasis. He frames marketing as helping others become who they want to be, and argues that great marketers use marketing to solve other people's problems rather than their company's.

The formal definitions tell you what the job covers. Godin's tells you what it is for. Keep both in mind and the helmet in this book stops being about a helmet and starts being about the rider. That idea comes back in chapter 7, where the top rung of the benefits ladder describes the person the buyer wants to become.

Where this book sits

The Manual Focus Basics series has separate guides on the vocabulary of promotion, including owned, paid and earned media, and above and below the line. This book covers what has to be true before you choose any channel. Get those decisions right and the channel choices get much easier.

Chapter 02

The common thread

Marketing is one chain of decisions. Each step answers one question and hands its answer to the next.

The order matters. Skip a step and the gap shows up later, usually as a campaign that can't explain why anyone should care. A team that writes the message before the research ends up promoting what the factory can make rather than what buyers need. A team that writes requirements without findings builds features looking for a reason.

The chain also maps onto SOSTAC, the planning model the last chapters use to run it. The first two steps are the Situation, the middle steps make up the Objectives, Strategy and Tactics, and the plan and its measures are Action and Control.

The helmet brief

A UK cycling brand wants to launch a new road helmet. It has a design team, a few retail stockists and an online shop. The leadership team has asked marketing for a go-to-market plan.

The rest of this book follows that brief from the first question to the last measure. Every name, finding and feature is invented for the example.

The tempting first move is to ask what the launch campaign should look like. This book starts somewhere less glamorous.

Chapter 03 · Step 1

Is there a market?

The first question is whether there is a market for the product, and whether you can win it.

The term people use is product-market fit. The definition most people quote comes from Marc Andreessen, in an essay called "The only thing that matters", published on his blog on 25 June 2007.

Product/market fit means being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market.Marc Andreessen, 2007

He went further and wrote that the only thing that matters is getting to product/market fit. He was writing about start-ups, but the logic carries to any launch. If the market isn't there, nothing downstream fixes it.

Why marketing belongs in this conversation

In many companies the product decision is made first, and marketing is brought in at launch and asked to sell it. By then the expensive choices are locked. Marketing should be in the room for this question, because it is the function whose job, by the institute's definition, is identifying and anticipating what customers need.

A market existing is not a market for you

For a helmet, a market plainly exists, because riders already buy helmets. That is the trap. The useful question is much narrower, and it has three parts.

  1. Which riders are badly served by what they wear now?
  2. Roughly how many of them are there?
  3. Would they switch at the price the brand needs to charge?

If the honest answer to the first is "nobody in particular", the launch should stop there. That is hard to say inside a company that has already designed the product, but it is the cheapest moment to say it, because every step after this costs more.

Instead of what?

The most useful question in this chapter is what those riders are doing instead. The competition is not only other helmets. For a rider who wants to be seen on a dark evening, the alternative might be their current helmet with a cheap clip-on light. It might be another brand's helmet. Or it might be not riding after dark at all.

Those are very different competitors. If the real alternative is staying at home, the launch has to give people a reason to ride, not a reason to switch brands, and the person staying at home appears in nobody's market share figures.

A hypothesis, not a verdict

You rarely get a clean yes or no at this stage. You get a hypothesis, such as "we think year-round riders are badly served", and the next step exists to test it. So the output of step one is a go or no-go on whether it is worth finding out more.

The helmet, step 1

The brand's working hypothesis is that riders who keep cycling through the winter and through hot summers are badly served by helmets designed for mild weekend rides. It decides that is worth testing before anything else is designed or briefed.

Who is badly served by what they use now, and how do you know?

What do they do instead? List at least three alternatives, including doing nothing.

Would enough of them switch at the price you need? What would change your mind?

Chapter 04 · Step 2

Market research

Research is where "cyclists" stop being a category and start being people.

For the helmet, the research needs to find out a handful of things.

What to find outWhy it matters for the launch
Who rides, and whereDefines the audience and the conditions the product must handle
What they wear now, and why they chose itShows the real competition and the reasons it wins
What they complain aboutPoints to the gap a new product could fill
Where they buyDecides place, and whether fitting help is part of the product
What triggers a purchaseTells you when people are ready to listen

Triggers, or category entry points

The triggers are the most useful part. The Manual Focus Lens has a playbook on category entry points, a framework from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for the moments and needs that bring someone into a category in the first place.

For a helmet you can guess at a few. A crash means the old one has to be replaced. A new bike prompts a new kit list. Someone starts commuting. The clocks go back and the evenings get dark. Each trigger brings a different person with a different worry, and a launch that knows which triggers it is going after will spend its money better.

The playbook pulls these triggers from four streams, which are search data, reviews, forum threads and a small set of interviews with eight to twelve recent buyers, about thirty minutes each. It takes about three working days, which is cheap compared with the cost of a launch aimed at the wrong person.

End in findings, not charts

The most common failure in research is a long deck of charts with no conclusions. Nobody can tell which facts matter, so the rest of the plan ignores them. Good research ends in three or four findings, each one a sentence, each with its evidence underneath.

The helmet, step 2

Say the hypothetical research produced three findings.

  1. Year-round commuters want drivers to see them on dark evenings.
  2. Road riders overheat on long climbs in summer.
  3. People riding for several hours find most helmets uncomfortable by the end.

These three findings shape everything that follows.

Choosing between people

The commuter on a dark evening and the road rider on a summer climb are often not the same person. That is a real decision. You can build a helmet for one of them and do it brilliantly, or try to serve both and risk serving neither. In many launches this is where the team quietly avoids choosing.

For the example, the brand looks for the thread between the groups. Both want to keep riding when conditions are against them, dark in one case and heat in the other. If the research backs that up, the launch can be built around year-round riding. If it doesn't, the brand should pick one group and say no to the other, at least for the first season.

AI and research · who is missing

AI is very good at summarising a pile of reviews or forum threads, but it can only summarise what is in the pile. Reviews come from people who bought something and felt strongly enough to write. You will hear a lot from existing buyers of other helmets and very little from people who don't ride in winter because they don't feel safe. Those people might be your best market, and they are not in the data. That is why the interviews matter, and why some of them should be with people who aren't buying yet.

Chapter 05 · Step 3

Product requirements

This is the step where marketing most often gets left out, which is odd, because product is the first of the 7Ps.

A requirements brief written by marketing describes what the product has to do for the buyer, in the buyer's words, and leaves engineering to decide how.

From the first finding, a requirement might be "visible from behind to drivers on an unlit road". An integrated rear light is one answer to it. A reflective shell or a light mount might be others. Choosing between them is engineering's job. The requirement is the problem, and the feature is one solution.

Every requirement traces back

Each requirement should trace to a research finding. Staying cool on a long climb traces to the second finding. Comfort on a five-hour ride traces to the third. Anything in the brief that traces to nothing is a feature looking for a reason.

That gives a useful test for any product meeting. When somebody wants to add a feature, ask which finding it is for. If nobody can answer, it probably shouldn't be there, or at least it shouldn't be in the launch story.

The test works in the other direction too. A finding with no requirement against it means the product is ignoring something the market told you, and that gap is often where the competitive advantage sits, in the thing everyone in the category knows riders complain about and nobody has fixed.

Price and place are requirements too

The brief should cover price and where the product is sold, because they come from the same findings. If research shows most riders buy their helmet in a bike shop where someone helps with the fit, then being stocked and fitted in shops is a requirement, and it changes the margin the brand needs. If they buy online, the fit system has to work without help. Product, price and place are one decision made from one picture of the buyer.

Chapter 06 · Step 4

The communication hierarchy

A communication hierarchy ranks what you say. One message comes first, and everything else supports it.

The structure has three levels.

  • One core message, the single thing you want a buyer to remember if they remember nothing else.
  • Three or four supporting messages beneath it, each one a reason to believe the core message.
  • Proof under each supporting message, the facts that make it true.

Why the ranking matters

Every channel has a different amount of room. A six-second video can carry one thing. A poster can carry one thing and perhaps a line beneath it. A product page can carry the whole hierarchy. So each channel leads with the top and goes as far down as it has room for. Without a ranking, every channel picks its own favourite message, and the launch says six different things.

The helmet, step 4

Working from the findings, the core message is "the helmet for riding all year". The supporting messages follow the research, seen on dark evenings, cool on long climbs and comfortable all day. The proof under each is the specification, the light, the vents and the fit system.

The core message is a choice. It says the helmet is for people who ride in the dark and the cold, and by implication not for someone who rides twice a summer.

Who owns it

In most teams the hierarchy sits with whoever owns positioning, usually brand or product marketing. What matters is that one person signs it off and everyone else works from it. The agency writing the ads, the person writing the emails and the retail team briefing shops should all have the same page. It belongs at the top of every brief, because a brief that doesn't say which message it carries will get back whatever the writer found most interesting.

Where it goes wrong

  • A core message that is really a list. "Light, cool, comfortable and great value" is four messages joined with commas.
  • A core message nobody could disagree with. "Quality you can trust" is true of every helmet brand's view of itself, so it tells the buyer nothing.

A useful check is the portability test. If a sentence would still be true about a different company, cut it or make it specific.

In the Lens · message house generator

The Manual Focus message house playbook builds this structure from a positioning brief. It produces one narrative sentence at the top, three or four pillars, three to five proof points under each pillar and lines for each channel. It adds a rebuttal sheet, eight to twelve of the hardest questions a sceptic will ask, each answered from the proof. For a helmet, the obvious one is "why should I pay more than I do now?". If the hierarchy can't answer that, it isn't ready.

Chapter 07 · Step 5

The benefits ladder

The hierarchy decides what you say. The benefits ladder decides how you say each part of it.

Most launch copy reads like a specification sheet. The benefits ladder fixes that. It has three rungs.

  • Feature, at the bottom. What the product has.
  • Benefit, in the middle. What the feature does for you.
  • Emotional resonance, at the top. Why that benefit matters to you as a person.

You take each supporting message and climb it.

FeatureBenefitEmotional resonance
Integrated rear lightDrivers see you on dark eveningsYou get home, and nobody at home has to worry
Larger vents and internal channelsCooler on long climbsYou ride further and feel strong doing it
Adjustable fit systemComfortable on a five-hour rideYou forget you're wearing it and enjoy the ride

Where the ladder comes from

The ladder draws on laddering, an interview method that Thomas Reynolds and Jonathan Gutman set out in the Journal of Advertising Research in 1988. Researchers still describe it as an in-depth, one-on-one interviewing technique for understanding how consumers translate the attributes of products into meaningful associations with respect to self. It sits within what researchers call the means-end chain.

In an interview, the researcher keeps asking "why does that matter to you?", and the answers climb from the product to the person. So research climbs the ladder by asking why, and copy uses the answers. The emotional rung should come from what people said in the interviews, not from a meeting. If riders said they worry about being seen in the dark, the copy can talk about getting home, because they told you so.

The top rung is also where Godin's definition from chapter 1 shows up in practice. It describes the rider the buyer wants to become.

The ladder works in both directions

Copy that stops at the bottom rung reads like a spec sheet. The opposite failure is a launch film that is all feeling, with no clue about what the product does, which people enjoy and can't connect to anything. The ladder guards against both, because every emotional line has to stand on a benefit and a feature. If you can't climb down from a line to the thing that proves it, the line doesn't belong in the launch.

A caution for safety products

The emotional rung can't promise more than the product does. Helping drivers see you is a fair claim for a rear light. Implying that a helmet keeps you safe from anything is not. The proof at the bottom has to hold up the promise at the top.

How the ladder and the hierarchy fit together

The core message often lives near the top of the ladder, because it is about the rider, not the helmet. The supporting messages sit at the benefit rung. The proof points are the features at the bottom. A short ad leads with the emotional line, and a product page can climb all the way down to the specification.

Chapter 08 · Step 6

SOSTAC brings it together

SOSTAC turns the chain into a plan that a team can run, review and change.

SOSTAC is the planning model created by the British marketer PR Smith in the 1990s. The letters stand for Situation, Objectives, Strategy, Tactics, Action and Control. PR Smith gives the stages plain questions. Where are we now? Where do we want to be? How do we get there? Tactics are the details of strategy, and actions are the details of tactics. And finally, how do we know we are getting there?

Much of the plan is already written by this point. Product-market fit and the research are the Situation.

Objectives

The objectives say what the launch must achieve, and by when. Sales, new customers or a share of a particular group of riders, each with a date. Tie them to business results rather than activity. "Launch the helmet with a big splash" is not an objective, because nobody can say whether it was met. Keep the business objective, such as revenue, separate from the marketing objectives that serve it, such as new customers or repeat purchase.

Strategy

Strategy is who you target, how you position the product against the alternatives, and which channels and partners carry it. For the helmet, the audience is the year-round rider, the position is the helmet for riding all year, and the communication hierarchy is the heart of the strategy, because it is the decision about what the brand stands for.

Tactics

Tactics are the marketing mix in detail. The price, where it is stocked, the launch content written with the benefits ladder, and the specific campaigns and channels. This is where owned, paid and earned media and the rest of the channel vocabulary come back in.

Action

Action is who does what, by when and with what budget. For a product with a season, the Manual Focus Lens has a gear launch sequence playbook built as a twelve-month calendar. It runs from pre-launch foundation work, through the hero shoot, a quieter phase before the reveal and the public reveal, then proof from riders and races, and finally a long stretch of sustained sell-through.

The playbook itself says the full calendar is overkill for a product with no athlete proof and no retail channel, and a small brand would run a much leaner version. The principle holds either way. Every piece of work has a name next to it and a date.

Control

Control is the measures you review against each objective, on a rhythm. A short monthly look and a proper quarterly review, where the team is allowed to change the tactics, suit most teams. For a launch, Control should reach right back to the start of the chain.

StepWhat to measure
Product-market fitWhether the group you aimed at is the group buying, from a short survey at purchase
ResearchWhether the triggers you planned around show up in why people say they bought
MessageWhether people can play back the core message when asked what the product is for
BusinessSales against the objective, and the margin

If the people buying are a different group from the one you planned for, the situation analysis was wrong, and you want to know that early. Control feeds the next Situation, which is what makes SOSTAC a loop rather than a document. The message playback check is cheap to run and tells you whether the hierarchy survived contact with the real channels, which most launches never check.

Words teams mix up

The Basics guide to strategy, campaign, creative and execution is worth reading alongside this chapter. The communication hierarchy is strategy. A launch campaign is a time-bound push that carries it. The benefits ladder feeds the creative, the idea people see. The launch calendar is execution. Teams that blur these end up blaming the idea for a missed deadline.

Where AI fits

There is work for AI at every step of the chain. The decisions don't move.

Three layers of AI

The Basics guide to the three layers of AI sorts these jobs further. Chat with a language model helps you draft the ladders. An agent, which takes a whole request and delivers a finished result, can run the research synthesis or check every piece of launch copy against the hierarchy. Orchestration is when several of those steps run together as one system.

A first draft only

PR Smith wrote about SOSTAC and AI for the Chartered Institute of Marketing in September 2025. He named situation analysis, strategy, tactics and actions as the stages where AI is especially relevant, and advised treating any AI-generated plan as a first draft only, checked against all six sections. He also argued that marketers need to become agile scientists, or at least experimentalists, testing and improving all the time.

Our view at Manual Focus is that AI makes this whole chain cheaper to run properly. The steps teams used to skip for lack of time, the research at the start and the measurement at the end, are exactly the ones AI makes affordable, so they deserve more attention now, not less. The order of the chain stays the same. You still start by asking whether there is a market.

Chapter 10

Get AI on this

Three jobs from the chain that you can hand to AI. Each prompt works in any capable assistant.

Replace the bracketed parts with your own brand, product and market, attach what each prompt asks for, and read the output as a first draft. Each prompt tells the model to say what it couldn't check rather than guess.

1. Turn your research into findings

Attach your review exports, a set of forum threads and your customer interview transcripts.

You are helping the marketing team at [brand], which sells [product category] in [market], prepare for the launch of [new product].

I've attached customer reviews, forum threads and interview transcripts. Read all of them and find:
1. The purchase triggers, the moments or needs that lead someone to buy in this category.
2. The complaints people have about what they use now.

Return a table with the columns: Trigger or complaint | How many sources mention it | Two verbatim quotes | Source file. Rank by how often it comes up.

Then write three or four findings, one sentence each, with the evidence from the table under each one.

Finally, list the groups of people the material doesn't cover (for example, people who don't buy in the category yet) and say what you couldn't check. Don't invent quotes or counts. If something appears only once, say so.

2. Draft a benefits ladder for every feature

Attach the product specification, your research findings and the interview transcripts.

You are helping the marketing team at [brand] write launch messaging for [new product].

I've attached the product specification, our research findings and customer interview transcripts.

For each feature in the specification, build a benefits ladder:
- Feature: the feature as specified.
- Benefit: what it does for the buyer, in plain words.
- Emotional resonance: two or three options for why that benefit matters to the buyer as a person.

Every emotional line must be tied to something a customer actually said in the transcripts. Quote the line it's based on. If no customer said anything that supports an emotional rung for a feature, say so rather than writing one.

Return a table with the columns: Feature | Benefit | Emotional options | Supporting quote | Research finding it traces to.

Flag any line that promises more than the feature can prove, and any feature that traces to no research finding.

3. Check launch copy against the hierarchy

Run it before each launch phase goes live. Attach the hierarchy and every piece of copy.

You are checking launch copy for [brand]'s [new product] against its communication hierarchy.

Attached are:
1. The communication hierarchy: one core message, three or four supporting messages and the proof points under each.
2. Every piece of launch copy: ads, emails, social posts, the product page.

For each piece of copy, return a row with the columns: Piece | Channel | Message it leads with | Which hierarchy level that is | Supporting messages it includes | Anything it says that isn't in the hierarchy.

Then list:
- Pieces that lead with something other than the core message, where the channel has room for only one message.
- Supporting messages that never appear in any piece.
- Claims in the copy that no proof point supports.

If you can't tell which message a piece leads with, say so rather than guessing.
Chapter 11

Where people go wrong

Most broken launches fail at one of these links in the chain.

MistakeWhat it looks likeThe fix
Starting at the endChannels and content chosen before anyone checked there's a marketAnswer the step 1 questions first
Research as a reportCharts with no conclusions, so no decision changesThree or four findings with evidence
Nobody choosesA product for two groups that serves neitherFind the thread between them, or pick one
A brief written in featuresNobody can say which problem each feature solvesRequirements in the buyer's words, traced to findings
A core message that is a listFour ideas joined with commas, a different one in every channelOne core message, signed off by one person
A bottom-rung ladderSpec-sheet copyClimb each feature to the benefit and the feeling
A top-rung ladderAn emotional line no customer said, with nothing to prove itTie every emotional line to a quote and a feature
No ControlThe plan is filed after launchMonthly and quarterly reviews that feed the next Situation

The most expensive mistake is treating all of this as a one-off. The research, hierarchy and ladder should be living documents, and when Control shows different buyers from the ones you planned for, go back up the chain.

Chapter 12

The one-page plan

The chain scales down. For a small brand or a single campaign, every step can be a paragraph. If any answer is blank, you are not ready to brief.

1. Is there a market, and who in it is badly served? What do they do instead?

2. What did we learn? Write three or four findings, each with its evidence.

3. What must the product do for them, in their words?

4. What is the one thing we say first?

5. Why does it matter to the buyer? Write the top rung of the ladder.

6. Who does what by when, and how will we know it's working?
