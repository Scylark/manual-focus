Public relations is the work of looking after an organisation's reputation. The Chartered Institute of Public Relations defines it as the discipline that looks after reputation, aiming to earn understanding and support and influence opinion and behaviour. Most PR works through earned media, coverage the brand can't buy, which is why relevance matters more than volume.

## Key takeaways

- The CIPR defines PR as the discipline which looks after reputation, with the aim of earning understanding and support and influencing opinion and behaviour.
- PR mostly works through earned media, where a third party decides whether to cover you, so it is less controllable and more credible than advertising.
- In Muck Rack's 2026 survey of 897 journalists, 86% said some of their stories start from PR pitches, and 88% immediately disregard pitches that miss their beat.
- Journalists named clear relevance, access to sources, original research and high-resolution images as the most valuable parts of a pitch.
- 82% of journalists in the same survey used at least one AI tool, so AI-drafted mass pitching now competes with a lot of noise.

## What is public relations?

The [CIPR's definition](https://www.cipr.co.uk/CIPR/About_Us/About_PR.aspx) is the clearest starting point. It describes public relations as the discipline which looks after reputation, with the aim of earning understanding and support and influencing opinion and behaviour, and as the planned and sustained effort to establish and maintain goodwill and mutual understanding between an organisation and its publics.

Two words in that definition do the work. "Earning" means PR can't buy the result, and "publics" is plural because an organisation has several audiences at once, including customers, staff, investors, regulators, local communities and the press.

## What does PR cover?

PR is wider than press coverage. Most in-house teams and agencies split it into a few disciplines.

| Discipline | What it covers | Typical output |
|---|---|---|
| Media relations | Working with journalists, editors and creators | Pitches, press releases, interviews, briefings |
| Corporate communications | The organisation's story to investors, partners and the public | Announcements, results commentary, leadership profiles |
| Internal communications | Keeping staff informed and aligned | Staff updates, leadership messages, change programmes |
| Crisis and issues management | Preparing for and handling bad news | Holding statements, response plans, spokesperson training |
| Public affairs | Engaging with government and regulators | Consultation responses, policy briefings |
| Thought leadership | Senior people's opinions in public | Opinion pieces, talks, podcasts, LinkedIn posts |

## How is PR different from advertising?

Advertising is paid media. The brand buys the space and controls every word. PR mostly works through earned media, where a journalist, creator or reviewer decides whether to cover you and what to say. You give up control, and in return you get credibility, because the audience knows the brand didn't pay for the words. The Basics guide to [owned, paid and earned media](/blog/owned-paid-earned-media-basics/) covers how the three types work together, and Gini Dietrich's [PESO model](https://spinsucks.com/communication/peso-model-breakdown/), popular with PR teams, adds shared social media as a fourth.

The line between the two blurs in sponsored content and paid influencer posts. Those are paid media, and in the UK the [CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/02.html) requires marketing communications to be obviously identifiable as such.

## What makes a story worth covering?

Journalists cover what is new, specific and relevant to their readers. In practice that tends to mean original data, a sharp opinion from someone senior with real expertise, a genuine change such as a launch or a decision, or a human story that shows a wider point.

Seth Godin's [Purple Cow](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/54880/purple-cow-by-godin-seth/9780141924861) argues that only remarkable products and ideas get noticed, and the same test applies to a story. If nobody outside the company would mention it to a friend, a press release won't change that. Our view at Manual Focus is that most PR problems are really product or story problems, so the first job is to find something remarkable to say before anyone writes a pitch.

## How do you pitch a journalist?

Relevance decides almost everything. Muck Rack's [State of Journalism 2026](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/03/19/3259178/0/en/muck-rack-s-2026-state-of-journalism-report-finds-82-of-journalists-use-ai.html) surveyed 897 journalists, mostly in the US with others from the UK, Canada and India. 86% said at least some of their stories start from PR pitches, but nearly half said they seldom receive pitches that match what they cover, and 88% immediately disregard pitches that miss their beat. 78% said a pitch feels relevant when it directly affects the community their audience belongs to.

The same journalists named the most valuable parts of a pitch as clear relevance to their beat, access to relevant sources, original research or data, and high-resolution images. That gives a simple checklist. Send it only to people who cover the subject, say why it matters to their readers in the opening lines, and have the spokesperson, data and images ready before you hit send. ## How has AI changed PR?

Both sides of the pitch now use AI. The Muck Rack survey found 82% of journalists use at least one AI tool, up from 77% the year before, while PR teams use AI to draft outreach at volume. The result is more well-written, irrelevant pitches, which makes careful targeting worth more.

AI also changes where coverage ends up. AI assistants draw on third-party coverage when they answer questions about a category, so earned media now shapes what those answers say about your brand. [Earned channels in the AI era](/blog/earned-channels-pr-in-the-ai-era/) covers this in depth, and [the citation moved off the page](/blog/the-citation-moved-off-the-page/) shows how far AI citations have drifted from Google rankings.

Some PR work should stay human. Senior opinion pieces and the voice of a founder are on our list of [places AI shouldn't touch](/blog/when-not-to-use-ai-in-marketing/), and the [founder and institutional voice](/lens/brand/founder-and-institutional-voice/) playbook sets out who speaks for the brand where.

## How do you measure PR?

Count outcomes, not volume. Useful measures include coverage in the outlets your audience reads, the share of coverage carrying your key messages, branded search after coverage lands, referral traffic, and whether AI assistants describe your brand accurately. Avoid advertising value equivalents, which put a price on coverage as if it were bought space and say nothing about whether anyone's opinion changed.
