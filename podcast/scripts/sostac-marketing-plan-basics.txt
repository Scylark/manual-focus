JAMES: Most marketing plans I'm shown start with tactics. A list of channels, a content calendar, a few campaigns, and somewhere near the bottom a line about objectives. There's a planning model that puts things back in the right order, and it's the one I come back to more than any other. HANNAH: Hi James. I'll admit this isn't an AI topic, which is unusual for me. But it turns out to be a very good frame for working out where AI belongs in a plan, so I'm glad we're doing it.

JAMES: We'll get there. Start with the basics. What is it and where does it come from?

HANNAH: SOSTAC is a planning model from a British marketer called PR Smith. He developed it in the nineteen-nineties and set it out in his book Marketing Communications in nineteen ninety-eight. He's been publishing SOSTAC guides himself since twenty eleven, and the tenth edition last year was billed as the AI edition. The letters stand for Situation, Objectives, Strategy, Tactics, Action and Control.

JAMES: And it's lasted.

HANNAH: It has. Smith's own site says the Chartered Institute of Marketing voted it one of the top three business models worldwide. I think the reason is simple. Each stage answers one plain question. Where are we now? Where do we want to be? How do we get there? What exactly will we do? Who does what, and when? And how do we know we're getting there?

JAMES: So take me through it the way a team would use it. Situation first.

HANNAH: Smith's guidance is that the situation analysis is about half of the whole plan. Customers, competitors, how you did against last year's objectives. You'll usually find a SWOT in here, and often a PESTEL, which scans the bigger picture from politics through to technology. He suggests putting the detail in an appendix and keeping only the key findings up front.

JAMES: Which is the bit most teams get wrong. The common failure is a long deck of charts with no conclusions. Nobody can tell which facts matter, so the rest of the plan ignores them. The test is whether it ends in three or four findings, each with its evidence. Something like, we're losing first-time buyers to a cheaper competitor, and here's the data that shows it.

HANNAH: And that's the slog AI is already good at. Pulling the data together, summarising competitor sites, synthesising research. Deciding which findings matter still sits with a person.

JAMES: Then objectives. Where do we want to be?

HANNAH: Smith recommends SMART objectives. Specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. He also has a second check he calls the five Ss, from when the internet arrived. Sell, serve, speak, save and sizzle. They're the five things digital can do for a business, so every objective should serve at least one.

JAMES: [thoughtfully] The discipline I'd add is tying them to business results. "Grow online revenue from new customers by fifteen per cent by next September" gives the plan a target. "Increase brand awareness" doesn't, because nobody can say whether you hit it.

HANNAH: Then the stage people argue about most. Strategy and tactics.

JAMES: Strategy is the route. Who you'll target, how you'll position the brand against the alternatives, which channels carry the plan. Smith makes a point I like a lot. Strategy is the shortest part of a plan and, in his experience, the weakest part of most plans.

HANNAH: He has a checklist for it too, doesn't he? TOPPP SEED.

JAMES: He does, and it starts where you'd expect, with target markets and positioning. Tactics are the details of that route, the marketing mix. The test I use is whether a line would still be true if every campaign changed. "Win cyclists who train indoors through the winter, through coaches and clubs rather than paid social." That survives a change of campaign, so it's strategy. "Run a January indoor training challenge" is a tactic that serves it.

HANNAH: That's a useful test. And it links to two earlier Basics episodes. The one on strategy, campaigns, creative and execution, and the one on owned, paid and earned media.

JAMES: Right. Those are two ways of thinking about the channels inside a strategy, and a plan should be clear on both. Then action, which is the bit most plans skip because it's dull.

HANNAH: Smith rates it the second weakest section, after strategy. He puts internal marketing here, meaning the briefing and training that help your own team and your agencies carry the tactics out well.

JAMES: In practice it's an owner's name against every piece, a timeline, a budget. The plans that fail in delivery nearly always fail here. Everyone agreed what to do, and nobody was named as doing it.

HANNAH: This is also where Smith's four Ms come in. The four resources every plan needs. He calls them men and women, money, minutes and megadata.

JAMES: [dryly] Megadata. Very nineties.

HANNAH: [chuckles] It is, but the point holds. People with the right skills, a budget, enough time and the data to run and measure it. Miss one of the four and the tactics don't happen, however good the strategy is.

JAMES: And then control. Are we getting there?

HANNAH: The measures for each objective, how often you report and the points where the team decides what to change. Smith describes a mini-loop here. What Control finds feeds straight back into the next situation analysis and the objectives.

JAMES: So control is where the plan gets updated, not just scored. In most teams I'd want a short monthly report against each objective and a proper review every ninety days, where you're allowed to change the tactics while the strategy stays put unless the situation has genuinely changed.

HANNAH: Let me make it concrete. The article has a worked example, and it's hypothetical, so nobody go looking for this brand. A small UK gravel bike brand launching a new frame in the spring.

JAMES: Go on.

HANNAH: Situation, most sales come from riders who already own one of its bikes, new riders find it through reviews in cycling media, and bigger brands outspend it on paid social. Objective, sell four hundred frames by the end of June, at least half of them to riders new to the brand.

JAMES: And the strategy follows from that situation. Target experienced gravel riders who race or ride events, and win them on fit and handling through event partners and reviewers, rather than fighting the big brands on paid social.

HANNAH: Tactics, demo bikes at four spring events, frames out to three review titles and an email series asking past customers for referrals. Action, the founder owns events and a freelancer owns email, with the demo bikes booked by February.

JAMES: And control is weekly frame sales, split by new and returning riders, plus orders that mention an event or a review. Reviewed at thirty, sixty and ninety days. Then in July you run the whole loop again.

HANNAH: That's the bit people miss. It's a loop, not a document.

JAMES: Which brings us to the honest assessment. What's good about SOSTAC and what isn't?

HANNAH: The good side is easy. Anyone can learn it in minutes, it forces evidence before tactics, and it works for a single campaign or a whole business plan. The weaker side is that it reads as a straight line when real planning loops back. It tells you what a strategy has to cover but not how to judge whether it's a good one. And plans written to fill every box can grow into long documents nobody reads.

JAMES: I'd add one. It assumes you know your objectives before you've tested anything. Most of those problems come from how teams use it, though. A one-page version with a ninety-day review fixes most of them.

HANNAH: People also ask how it compares with RACE, which is Smart Insights' customer journey framework. Dave Chaffey shows the two working together. SOSTAC gives the plan its order, and RACE gives the tactics stage a way to cover each step of the journey.

JAMES: And when wouldn't you use it?

HANNAH: For a small test you could run in a week, it adds very little. And if you don't yet know who your customer is, do the research first and come back to SOSTAC once the situation analysis has something real in it.
