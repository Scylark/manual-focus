JAMES: Every channel a brand uses is one of three things. Something it owns, something it pays for, or something it earns. That split has been standard vocabulary for well over a decade, and it's still the quickest way I know to see what's wrong with a media plan. HANNAH: Owned media is any channel the brand controls. Your website, your email list, your app, your blog, this podcast. Paid media is attention you buy. Search ads, social ads, display, television, radio, outdoor, sponsorship. And earned media is what other people choose to say or share about you. Press coverage, reviews, mentions, word of mouth, and these days, citations in AI answers.

JAMES: And where does the framework come from? It feels like it's always been there.

HANNAH: It became standard through a Forrester report in December two thousand and nine, by an analyst called Sean Corcoran, called No Media Should Stand Alone. The title is the argument. The summary says all three can play specific roles in meeting marketers' objectives, especially when they work together. And it uses a lovely image. Paid media as a catalyst, and owned media built out into a solar system of long-term touchpoints.

JAMES: The solar system is still the best way to picture it, I think. Owned at the centre, paid pushing attention towards it, and earned orbiting round it in conversations you don't control.

HANNAH: So take owned first. What does it look like in a real team?

JAMES: The website, the email list and the app, mostly. The thing that makes owned media valuable is that you keep the audience. Every subscriber is someone you can reach again without paying a platform or asking its permission. And the value builds over time, which is also its weakness. It's slow.

HANNAH: This is where Seth Godin comes in, with permission marketing.

JAMES: Explain it from the start, because people often think it just means opt-in boxes.

HANNAH: Godin wrote a book called Permission Marketing, and he summed the idea up in a blog post in two thousand and eight. He describes it as the privilege, not the right, of delivering messages that are anticipated, personal and relevant to people who want to get them. So it's not the tick box. It's whether the person is actually expecting to hear from you and glad when you turn up.

JAMES: And that's the useful test for an email list. A smaller list of people who look forward to your emails is worth more than a big one of people who never asked. In most teams the opposite happens. The list grows through competitions and checkout boxes, and then everyone wonders why open rates keep falling.

HANNAH: And the AI-era version of owned?

JAMES: That was the argument in the owned channels piece. The website should behave like a product that ships and tests every week, not something you rebuild every few years. HANNAH: Then paid.

JAMES: Paid is attention on demand. Its strength is control over timing and reach. You can be in front of a chosen audience tomorrow. Its weakness is that the reach stops when the money stops. It's also where the above-the-line and below-the-line split mostly lives, from mass television to tightly targeted retargeting, which is our other Basics episode on channels.

HANNAH: And there's a model that solves that by adding a fourth type.

JAMES: And media planners mostly stick with three. Either works, as long as the team uses one consistently.

HANNAH: I can see why PR people like the fourth type, though. A comment thread under your post behaves differently from your own post, and differently again from a journalist's article.

JAMES: It does. It's faster, less controllable and much closer to word of mouth. If your team spends a lot of time there, PESO probably fits better.

HANNAH: I'd add one thing on rented. The platform can change the rules whenever it likes.

JAMES: That's the risk in one sentence. You can spend years building a following on a platform and then watch its reach fall when the platform decides to show fewer brand posts. Which is another argument for treating every social profile as a way to bring people back to something you own, rather than as the destination.

HANNAH: And how do you measure each of the three? I'd assume they can't share one scorecard.

JAMES: They can't, and trying to is how earned and owned lose budget arguments. Owned is best measured by the audience you can reach again, subscribers, logged-in users, returning visitors. Paid by cost per result against the objective. And earned by the quality of the coverage and who's citing you, including whether AI answers describe the brand accurately. Then you review the three together, because each one feeds the others.

HANNAH: So how should the three work together?

JAMES: Use paid and earned to grow owned. A paid campaign that builds the email list keeps paying off after the budget stops. Coverage that links to a genuinely useful page on your site brings readers you can keep. And good owned content, clear and well sourced, gives journalists and AI answers something to cite. So the loop runs both ways.

HANNAH: And where do most brands get that wrong?

JAMES: Our view is that most brands under-invest in owned because its returns are slow, and then overspend on paid to make up for an audience they never built. So start any audit with owned, and judge every paid and earned activity partly by how much owned audience it leaves behind.
