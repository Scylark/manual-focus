The first episode of the Manual Focus Basics series. James and Hannah explain
the three layers of AI from first principles: large language models and chat,
agents that take a whole request and deliver it, and orchestration, where
several agents run as one system. Hannah covers how a language model predicts
text, why the context window matters and why models can be confidently wrong,
then Anthropic's distinction between workflows and agents and its published
results and costs for a multi-agent system. James grounds each layer in how
marketing teams work, why the dull jobs make the best first agent jobs, and
why orchestration is a leadership question.

## Get AI on this

Paste these into whichever AI assistant you use: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or
Copilot. Swap the bracketed parts for your own details. They work with pasted
text or uploaded files.

### 1. Sort your team's jobs into the three layers

```text
I lead marketing at [company], a team of [number] people. I want to know which
of our recurring jobs could be done by AI chat, by an AI agent, or by an
orchestrated system of several agents.

The three layers, as I use them:
- Layer 1, chat: a person prompts a language model and carries the work
  between turns.
- Layer 2, agent: the AI takes a whole request, uses tools such as files,
  analytics or a browser, and returns a finished piece of work.
- Layer 3, orchestration: several AI steps or agents run in sequence or in
  parallel as one system, with a person approving the final output.

Attached (or below) is a list of our recurring jobs, with who does each one,
how often and roughly how long it takes: [paste list].

Give me a table with the columns: Job | Owner | Hours per month | Best layer
(1, 2 or 3) | Why | What the AI would need access to | The check a person
should make before the output is used.

Then name the three jobs to delegate to an agent first, favouring repeatable
jobs with an obvious finished state. If a job needs a human decision or a
sign-in you can't see, say so in its row rather than guessing.
```

### 2. Write the brief for your first agent job

```text
I want to hand one recurring marketing job to an AI agent: [job, for example
the weekly paid channel report].

Here is what the job involves today: [describe the steps].
The data it should use is: [name the exports or systems, and which to trust
if they disagree].
A finished result looks like this: [describe or attach a good example].

Write the full instructions for the agent, in plain language, with these
sections: Goal | Inputs and where they come from | Steps | What finished looks
like | Where to stop and ask a person | What not to touch.

Keep it under one page. Mark anything you had to assume in [brackets] so I
can confirm it, and don't invent data sources we haven't named.
```

### 3. Write the checks before the agent runs

```text
I'm about to have an AI agent produce [job] for my marketing team every
[week/month]. Before it runs, I want a short checklist its output must pass.

Attached are two examples of a good [report/brief/audit] and one that went
wrong, with a note on what was wrong with it.

Write a checklist of no more than ten checks, each one specific enough that a
person, or a second AI, could mark it pass or fail in under a minute. Group
them under Accuracy, Completeness and Brand. For each check, say what evidence
in the output proves it passed.

If the examples don't show what "good" means for something important, say so
and suggest the question I should answer, rather than making up a standard.
```
