SOSTAC is a six-stage marketing planning model created by the UK marketer PR Smith in the 1990s. The letters stand for Situation, Objectives, Strategy, Tactics, Action and Control. Each stage answers one question, from where are we now to how do we know we are getting there, so a team can plan a campaign or a year the same way.

## Key takeaways

- SOSTAC was created by PR Smith in the 1990s and set out in his 1998 book Marketing Communications.
- Situation analysis should take about half the plan, while strategy is the shortest section and usually the weakest.
- Smith's 4Ms, the four resources every plan needs, decide whether it can actually be delivered.
- Control feeds the next situation analysis, so SOSTAC works best as a 90-day loop rather than a yearly document.
- The free one-page template below fits a single campaign or a whole year.

## What is SOSTAC?

SOSTAC is a planning checklist in the order you should think. PR Smith's [own summary](https://prsmith.org/sostac/) gives each stage a question. Those questions stop a team jumping straight to tactics, which is where most marketing conversations start. Smith's site says the Chartered Institute of Marketing voted it one of the top three business models worldwide.

| Stage | Question it answers | What goes in it | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation | Where are we now? | Customers, competitors, last year's results, key findings | A data dump with no conclusions |
| Objectives | Where do we want to be? | Measurable goals with dates, tied to business results | Activity goals such as "post three times a week" |
| Strategy | How do we get there? | Target audience, positioning, channel choices | A list of tactics called a strategy |
| Tactics | What exactly will we do? | The marketing mix, campaigns, content, pricing | Tactics that don't trace back to the strategy |
| Action | Who does what, when? | Owners, timelines, budgets, briefs | No named owner for each piece |
| Control | Are we getting there? | Measures, reporting rhythm, review points | Measuring what's easy instead of what matters |

## Who invented SOSTAC?

PR Smith developed SOSTAC in the 1990s. According to [Wikipedia's account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SOSTAC), he formalised it in his 1998 book Marketing Communications. His self-published SOSTAC guides followed from 2011, with a tenth edition in 2025 that covers AI. The digital marketing author Dave Chaffey later applied it to digital plans in his own books, which is why you'll often see the two names together.

## Where are we now?

Situation analysis is the evidence base for everything after it. Smith's guidance is that it takes about half of a plan. You can keep the detail in an appendix with only the key findings at the front.

It covers your customers and your competitors before moving on to your own results against last year's objectives. Two familiar tools live here. SWOT sums up strengths and weaknesses against outside opportunities and threats. PESTEL scans the wider context, from politics to technology.

The test of a good situation analysis is that it ends in conclusions. Five pages of charts with no "so what" leave the team to guess which facts matter. Three or four clear findings give the objectives something to stand on. The [positioning audit pipeline](/lens/brand/positioning-audit-pipeline/) and [Category Entry Point research](/lens/demand/category-entry-points/) playbooks both produce situation evidence a team can plan from.

## How do you set SOSTAC objectives?

Objectives say where you want to be. Smith recommends SMART objectives, meaning specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. The useful discipline is to tie them to business results. "Grow online revenue from new customers by 15% by next September" gives the plan a target. "Increase brand awareness" doesn't, because nobody can tell whether it was met.

Smith's 5Ss offer a second check. Sell, serve, speak, save and sizzle are the five things digital marketing can do for a business, so each objective should serve at least one of them.

## How do strategy and tactics differ?

Strategy is the route. It says who you will target and how you will position the brand against competitors. It also picks the channels and partners that carry the plan. Smith calls it the shortest part of a plan yet the weakest part of most plans he reviews. His TOPPP SEED checklist covers what a strategy should address, starting with target markets and positioning.

Tactics are the details of that route, the marketing mix that puts the strategy into the market. One way to check the difference is to ask whether the item would still be true if every campaign changed. "Win cyclists who train indoors through the winter, through coaches and clubs rather than paid social" survives a change of campaign, so it is strategy. "Run a January Strava challenge" is a tactic that serves it.

The Basics piece on [strategy, campaigns, creative and execution](/blog/strategy-campaign-creative-execution-basics/) goes further into where each word starts and stops. The channel choices inside a strategy are covered in [owned vs paid vs earned media](/blog/owned-paid-earned-media-basics/) and [above the line vs below the line](/blog/above-the-line-below-the-line-basics/).

## What goes in Action and Control?

Action turns tactics into work. It names an owner for each piece and sets the timeline. Then it allocates the budget. Smith also puts internal marketing here, meaning the briefing and training that help your team and agencies carry out the tactics well. He rates Action the second weakest section of most plans.

The 4Ms belong at this stage. Smith lists them as men and women, money, minutes and megadata. In plain terms you need people with the right skills plus a budget. You also need time for the work and data to run and measure it. If one of the four is missing, the tactics won't happen however good the strategy is.

Control says how you will know whether it's working. It sets the measures for each objective along with the reporting rhythm and review points. Smith describes a mini-loop that links Control back to Situation and Objectives. What Control finds becomes the next situation analysis, so the plan keeps learning. The [attribution tear-down](/lens/demand/attribution-teardown/) playbook tests whether the numbers in your dashboard reflect what actually drove results.

## What are SOSTAC's pros and cons?

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Anyone can learn the six stages in minutes | It reads as a straight line, though real planning loops back |
| It forces evidence before tactics | It shows what a strategy must cover but not how to judge one |
| One model fits a campaign or a whole business plan | Plans written to fill every box can grow long and unread |
| A shared structure makes plans easy to review | Situation analysis can swallow the time meant for decisions |
| Control builds learning into the next plan | It assumes you know your objectives before testing anything |

Most of the disadvantages come from how teams use SOSTAC rather than the model itself. A one-page version with a 90-day review keeps the loop tight. Testing a launch before committing budget answers the last point, which is what the [Rider Panel](/rider-panel/) was built for.

## How does SOSTAC compare with RACE?

RACE is Smart Insights' framework for the customer journey, from reaching people to keeping them engaged. Dave Chaffey [shows the two working together](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/sostac-marketing-plan-model). SOSTAC gives the plan its order. RACE gives the Tactics stage a way to cover each step of the journey. Use SOSTAC when you need a whole plan and RACE when you need to fix one part of the funnel.

## When should you not use SOSTAC?

SOSTAC is a planning model, so it adds little to a small test you could run in a week. It also struggles when you don't yet know who your customer is. In that case start with market research, then come back to SOSTAC once the situation analysis has something real in it.

## A free SOSTAC template

Copy this table into a document and fill in the last column. Keep each answer to two or three lines. If any row is blank, the plan isn't ready to brief.

| Stage | Prompt | Your plan |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | The three or four findings this plan responds to | |
| Objectives | One business objective plus the marketing objectives behind it, with dates | |
| Strategy | Who you target, the position you take, the main channels | |
| Tactics | The campaigns and content that carry the strategy this quarter | |
| Action | Owner, deadline and budget for each tactic, plus the 4Ms check | |
| Control | The number you judge each objective by, who reports it, when you review | |

## A worked SOSTAC example

This example is hypothetical. A small UK gravel bike brand wants to launch a new frame in spring.

| Stage | What the plan says |
|---|---|
| Situation | Most sales come from riders who already own one of the brand's bikes. New riders find the brand through reviews in cycling media. Larger brands outspend it on paid social. |
| Objectives | Sell 400 frames by the end of June, with at least half to riders who are new to the brand. |
| Strategy | Target experienced gravel riders who race or ride events. Position the frame on fit and handling at events, through event partners and reviewers rather than paid social. |
| Tactics | Demo bikes at four spring events, review frames for three titles, an email series to past customers asking for referrals. |
| Action | The founder owns events, a freelancer owns email, the budget is split by tactic, demo bikes are booked by February. |
| Control | Weekly frame sales split by new and returning riders, plus orders that name an event or review. Review at day 30, 60 and 90. |

Run the same loop again in July. What Control found becomes the next Situation.

## How does AI change SOSTAC?

PR Smith addressed this himself in a [September 2025 article for the CIM](https://www.cim.co.uk/content-insights/articles/sostac-plans-in-the-ai-age/), alongside an AI edition of his SOSTAC guide. He named four stages where AI is especially relevant, every stage except Objectives and Control. He also advised treating any AI-generated plan as a first draft only.

Mapped to the [three layers of AI](/blog/three-layers-of-ai-basics/), the split looks like this.

| Stage | Where AI helps | What stays with a person |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Pulling and summarising data, competitor reviews | Deciding which findings matter |
| Objectives | Modelling what different targets would require | Choosing the targets and owning them |
| Strategy | Stress-testing options and spotting gaps | The choice of audience and position |
| Tactics | Drafting campaign ideas and briefs | Picking which ideas to back |
| Action | Building timelines and recurring reports | Accountability for delivery |
| Control | Weekly reporting and anomaly checks | Deciding what to change |

Our view at Manual Focus is that AI makes SOSTAC more useful. It removes the hours that once made a thorough situation analysis and a proper control loop too expensive for most teams.
